Module 3

Building a Budget That Actually Works

A budget isn't a restriction. It's a plan. And plans win.

The Core Truth

Most people don't have a spending problem. They have a planning problem. When you tell your money where to go before the month starts, it goes there. When you don't, it disappears. A budget is simply a spending plan, and every person who's ever built wealth had one.

Why Budgets Fail (And How Yours Won't)

Budgets fail for four reasons: they're too rigid, they don't account for irregular expenses, they aren't reviewed regularly, or they're built on unrealistic numbers. Most budget templates set people up to quit within 30 days because they don't match how real life works.

The approach in this module is different. It's built around the actual dollars coming into your household each month, your real expenses (not ideal ones), and a system flexible enough to hold up when life happens, because life always happens.

Step 1: Know Your Real Take-Home Income

Start with what actually lands in your bank account. Not your gross salary. Not what you think you make. The exact dollars that hit your checking account each pay period.

If you're salaried, this is straightforward. If you're hourly, commission-based, or have variable income, take your average monthly take-home from the last three months. Use the lower end if you want a conservative plan.

Add all income sources: primary job, side income, child support received, any consistent money that comes in. This total is your starting number.

Action: Find Your Real Number

Pull up your bank statements from the last three months. Add up every dollar that came in. Divide by three. That's your monthly income figure for this budget. Be honest. This number has to be real for the plan to work.

Step 2: Map Every Expense

This step is where most people get uncomfortable, and it's exactly why they need to do it. You can't control what you can't see. Go through your last 60-90 days of bank and credit card statements and categorize everything that went out.

Fixed Expenses (Same Amount Every Month)

  • Rent or mortgage payment
  • Car payment
  • Insurance premiums (auto, life, renters/homeowners)
  • Phone bill
  • Internet
  • Minimum debt payments (credit cards, student loans, personal loans)
  • Subscriptions you'll actually keep

Variable Expenses (Change Month to Month)

  • Groceries
  • Gas and transportation
  • Utilities (electric, gas, water)
  • Eating out and entertainment
  • Clothing and personal care
  • Medical copays and prescriptions

Irregular Expenses (Don't Forget These)

This category destroys more budgets than any other because people forget about it. These are the expenses that don't show up every month but they show up every year and they're predictable.

  • Car registration and maintenance
  • Annual insurance payments
  • Holiday gifts and travel
  • Back-to-school costs
  • Birthdays and anniversaries
  • Home repairs and maintenance

Add up all your irregular annual expenses and divide by 12. That monthly figure needs a line in your budget. When you build it in, these "unexpected" expenses stop wrecking you because they're no longer unexpected.

The RSM Budget Framework: The 50/20/30 Rule (Modified)

The classic 50/30/20 budget rule puts 50% toward needs, 30% toward wants, and 20% toward savings and debt. We modify it based on where you are in your financial journey.

CategoryStandardDebt Payoff ModeWealth Build Mode
Needs (housing, food, transport, utilities)50%50%45%
Wants (dining, entertainment, lifestyle)30%15%25%
Savings & Investing20%15%30%
Extra Debt Payoff0%20%0%

These are starting points, not commandments. If your housing costs 55% of your income, that's your reality right now. Work with what's real. The goal is to get the percentages trending in the right direction over time, not to be perfect starting on day one.

Zero-Based Budgeting: Every Dollar Has a Job

Zero-based budgeting means that at the end of the month, your income minus your planned expenses equals zero. That doesn't mean you spend everything. It means every dollar is assigned somewhere, including savings, investments, and debt payoff. Nothing is left unaccounted for because unaccounted-for dollars disappear.

Income: $4,200
Total planned expenses and savings: $4,200
Remaining unallocated: $0

When you have a surplus after assigning everything, assign it intentionally. Add it to your emergency fund. Put it toward debt. Move it to savings. Just don't leave it floating in your checking account where it'll get spent on things you can't remember by the end of the week.

Step 3: Build the Actual Budget

Now you combine what you've learned. Take your real monthly income. Subtract every expense category. Assign every remaining dollar to savings, investing, or extra debt payoff. Do this before the month starts, not after it's over.

Automating Your Budget

The best budget system is one you don't have to remember to execute every day. Automation removes the willpower requirement entirely. Here's the automation sequence that works:

  1. Payday: Your paycheck hits your primary checking account.
  2. Automatic transfers fire: Set up automatic transfers to your savings account, emergency fund, and investment accounts on the day after payday.
  3. Bills auto-pay: Every fixed bill is set to autopay from your checking account on its due date.
  4. What's left: The remaining money in your checking is your spending money for the pay period. When it's gone, it's gone.

This structure means saving and investing happen first. You're not trying to save what's left over after spending. You're spending what's left over after saving. That one shift is the difference between building wealth and wondering where the money went.

Budget Categories That Most People Miss

Beyond the obvious categories, here are the ones that trip people up and cause budget blowouts:

  • Personal care: Haircuts, nail appointments, hygiene products. These add up to hundreds per month for most people.
  • Pet expenses: Food, vet visits, grooming, medication. If you have pets, you need a budget line for them.
  • Children's activities: Sports, school events, field trips, birthday parties. Separate from the grocery budget.
  • Technology replacements: Phones, laptops, and devices all eventually need replacing. Start a sinking fund.
  • Professional development: Courses, certifications, books, conferences. This one pays you back, so don't cut it.

When the Budget Doesn't Balance

If your expenses exceed your income when you map it all out, you have two options: increase income or decrease expenses. There's no third door. But here's how to think about which expenses to cut first.

Look at every line in your budget through this filter: Is this expense building my future or funding my past? Debt payments fund your past. Subscriptions you forgot about fund nothing. Entertainment, dining out, and lifestyle choices are places where cuts are most available without affecting your quality of life significantly if you're intentional about them.

Don't cut savings. Don't cut your emergency fund contribution. Don't cut investments, especially if you have an employer match available. Those are the expenses that build the future version of your life.

The Sinking Fund Strategy

A sinking fund is a savings account (or bucket within your savings account) dedicated to a specific future expense. Instead of being blindsided by irregular costs, you fund them in advance.

Examples of sinking funds worth having:

  • Car repairs and maintenance ($50-100/month)
  • Holiday gifts and travel ($50-150/month depending on your traditions)
  • Medical and dental ($30-75/month)
  • Home repairs if you own your home ($100-200/month)
  • Annual expenses: registration, subscriptions, memberships

High-yield savings accounts are perfect for sinking funds. Your money earns interest while it waits, and the slight friction of transferring money out makes you think twice before raiding the fund for something else.

Your Assignment Before Module 4

Build your first monthly budget using the steps in this module. Use real numbers from your actual bank statements. Assign every dollar. Identify the one biggest area where money is leaking out without intention. That's your first target. You don't have to fix everything in month one. But you have to see it first.
