Day 3 | Lesson 1

Goals, Risk Tolerance, and Time Horizon

The right investment depends on what the money is for, when you'll need it, and how you'll handle the ride.

There's no single "best investment." There's only the best fit for your goal, your timeline, and your ability to stay calm when prices fall. Today you start building a framework around those three things.

Start With the Goal

Every dollar you invest should have a job. Vague goals like "be rich" don't guide decisions. Specific goals do.

Vague GoalSpecific Goal
"Retire someday.""Retire at 62 with enough invested to replace part of my income."
"Save for a house.""Have a $20,000 down payment in four years."
"Help my kids.""Invest $50 a month for each grandchild until they turn 18."

Time Horizon: When You'll Need the Money

Time HorizonGeneral GuidelineWhy
Under 3 yearsKeep it in stable options like insured savings, CDs, or Treasury bills.There may not be time to recover from a market drop.
3 to 10 yearsA balanced mix of stocks and bonds is often used.Some growth potential with more stability.
10+ yearsGrowth-oriented investments like broad stock funds are commonly used.Time to ride out volatility and let compounding work.

Risk Tolerance vs. Risk Capacity

  • Risk tolerance is emotional: how you'd feel and act if your investments dropped 20% or 30%. Would you hold, buy more, or sell?
  • Risk capacity is financial: how much loss you could actually afford without it affecting your goals or daily life.

Your plan should respect the lower of the two. Someone with a long time horizon may have high risk capacity, but if they'd panic and sell in a downturn, a more moderate mix they can stick with may serve them better.

Common Rules of Thumb

You may hear shortcuts like "subtract your age from 110 to get your stock percentage." These are general starting points used for education, not personal advice. Target-date funds apply a similar idea automatically, shifting from mostly stocks toward more bonds as the target year approaches. Your real mix should reflect your goals, timeline, and tolerance.

Real-Life Case Study: Gloria and Devon, Mother and Son

Gloria, 58, plans to retire in 7 years and can't afford a big loss right before she needs the money. Devon, her son, is 27 and investing for retirement 35+ years away. Same family, same values, very different plans. Gloria uses a balanced mix and keeps two years of expected withdrawals in stable accounts as she gets closer. Devon uses a broad, low-cost stock index fund and a target-date fund in his 401(k), and ignores daily market news. Both are right, for their own situation.

Right Side of Money Principle

Your best investment plan isn't the one with the highest possible return. It's the one you'll actually stick with through the ups and downs.

Investment Accounts and Retirement Basics

Where you invest can matter as much as what you invest in.

An investment account is the container that holds your investments. Different containers come with different tax benefits and rules. Choosing the right container can save you thousands in taxes over time. The account isn't the investment. It's the house the investment lives in.

Workplace Retirement Plans

Plans like the 401(k) (private employers), 403(b) (schools, hospitals, nonprofits), and the Thrift Savings Plan, or TSP (federal employees and service members), let you invest directly from your paycheck.

  • Employer match: Many employers match part of what you contribute. Not taking the full match is leaving part of your pay on the table. Members of the military's Blended Retirement System can also receive matching TSP contributions.
  • Vesting: Employer contributions may take time to fully become yours. Check your plan's vesting schedule.
  • 2026 limit: Employees can contribute up to $24,500. Those 50 and older can add up to $8,000 more, and under current rules, those age 60 to 63 may be able to add up to $11,250 instead, if their plan allows.

Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs)

An IRA is a retirement account you open yourself at a brokerage. For 2026, you can contribute up to $7,500, plus an extra $1,100 if you're 50 or older, as long as you have earned income at least equal to what you contribute.

Traditional vs. Roth

Traditional (401(k) or IRA)Roth (401(k) or IRA)
Taxes on contributionsOften tax-deductible or pre-tax nowPaid with after-tax money
Taxes on qualified withdrawalsTaxed as income in retirementTax-free if rules are met (generally age 59½ and the account open at least five years)
Often considered byPeople who expect a lower tax rate in retirementPeople who expect a similar or higher tax rate later, often younger earners

Roth IRAs have income limits. For 2026, the ability to contribute phases out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and between $242,000 and $252,000 for married couples filing jointly. Withdrawing earnings from retirement accounts before age 59½ generally triggers taxes and a 10% penalty, with some exceptions.

Other Accounts Worth Knowing

  • Taxable brokerage account: No contribution limits and no withdrawal restrictions, but no special tax advantages. Useful for goals before retirement.
  • Health Savings Account (HSA): If you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan, HSAs can offer tax advantages going in, while invested, and coming out for qualified medical expenses.
  • 529 education savings plan: Tax-advantaged investing for education costs.
  • Custodial accounts (UTMA/UGMA): Accounts an adult manages for a child until they reach adulthood. A powerful way to start the next generation early.

A Common Order of Operations

  1. Contribute enough to get your full employer match.
  2. Pay off high-interest debt.
  3. Consider an IRA or Roth IRA, and an HSA if eligible.
  4. Increase workplace plan contributions.
  5. Use a taxable brokerage account for additional goals.

Contribution limits and tax rules change and depend on your situation. Confirm current rules at IRS.gov or with a qualified tax professional before making decisions.

Right Side of Money Principle

Before you ask "what should I buy?", ask "which account should it live in?" The right container keeps more of your growth in your family.

Automatic Investing, Dollar-Cost Averaging, and Rebalancing

The best investing system is one that runs without you having to make brave decisions.

Most investing mistakes come from emotion: buying when excited, selling when scared, waiting for the "right time." The solution is to take emotion out of the process with three simple systems.

1. Automatic Investing

Set a fixed amount to move from your paycheck or bank account into your investment account on a schedule, every payday or every month. Workplace plans do this automatically through payroll. For IRAs and brokerage accounts, most platforms let you set up recurring contributions and automatic purchases.

Automation means you invest whether the news is good or bad, whether you feel confident or nervous. That consistency is where long-term results come from.

2. Dollar-Cost Averaging

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing the same dollar amount on a regular schedule regardless of price. When prices are high, your money buys fewer shares. When prices fall, it buys more. Here's a simplified example of investing $100 a month:

MonthAmount InvestedShare PriceShares Bought
1$100$1010.0
2$100$812.5
3$100$520.0
4$100$812.5
5$100$1010.0
Total$500Average price $8.2065.0 shares

The price ended right where it started, at $10, yet the investor's 65 shares are worth $650. Their average cost per share was about $7.69, lower than the average price, because the fixed amount bought more shares when prices were low. Simplified illustration. DCA doesn't guarantee a profit or protect against loss in a declining market.

If you ever have a large lump sum, research has shown that investing it all at once has historically come out ahead more often than spreading it out, because markets have risen more often than they've fallen. But for most people investing from each paycheck, DCA is simply how investing works, and it builds the habit of buying through the ups and downs.

3. Rebalancing

Over time, different investments grow at different speeds and your mix drifts. Suppose you chose 70% stocks and 30% bonds. After a strong year for stocks, you might have 80% stocks and 20% bonds, which is more risk than you intended.

Rebalancing brings you back to your target, either by selling some of what grew and buying what lagged, or by directing new contributions to the part that's underweight.

  • Calendar approach: Check once or twice a year on set dates.
  • Threshold approach: Rebalance when any part drifts more than a set amount, often 5 percentage points.

In retirement accounts, rebalancing generally doesn't trigger taxes. In taxable accounts, selling can create taxable gains, so directing new money is often a cleaner option. Target-date funds rebalance for you automatically.

Right Side of Money Principle

Discipline beats brilliance. Automate it, keep buying through the ups and downs, and check your balance on a schedule, not on a feeling.

Day 3 | Lesson 4

Basic Investment Research

You don't need to be an expert. You need to know what to check and what to avoid.

Research doesn't mean reading financial news all day or following investing influencers. For most long-term investors, research means answering a few key questions about any fund, account, or professional before you put money in.

Researching a Fund

Pull up the fund's fact sheet or look it up at your brokerage. Check these seven things:

CheckQuestion to Ask
ObjectiveWhat is this fund trying to do? Match an index? Produce income? Beat the market?
HoldingsWhat does it actually own, and how many investments? Is it diversified?
Expense ratioWhat does it cost each year?
Loads and feesAre there sales charges or other fees?
Index or benchmarkWhat is it measured against, and how has it compared over long periods?
Risk levelHow much has it dropped in bad years?
FitDoes it match my goal, time horizon, and risk tolerance?

Look at long-term performance, 10 years or more when available, and remember that past performance doesn't guarantee future results. A fund that was the "top performer" last year often isn't the next year.

Researching a Financial Professional

  • FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org) shows a broker's registrations, employment history, and any disciplinary actions.
  • SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (adviserinfo.sec.gov) lets you look up registered investment advisers.
  • Ask directly: "Are you a fiduciary at all times when working with me?" A fiduciary is required to act in your best interest. Also ask, "How are you paid?" Fees, commissions, or both?

Where to Learn From Reliable Sources

  • Investor.gov, the SEC's free education site, including a compound interest calculator.
  • FINRA.org investor education.
  • Your brokerage's fund pages, fact sheets, and prospectuses.

Social Media Red Flags

Be very careful with investing advice from social media, group chats, and videos. Watch for:

  • Screenshots of huge gains with no losses shown
  • "Guaranteed" returns or "can't lose" strategies
  • Urgency: "Get in before it explodes"
  • Paid courses or groups selling a secret system
  • Anyone asking you to send money to them directly to invest
Real-Life Case Study: Brian, the Hot Tip

Brian saw a video of someone turning $500 into $40,000 on a little-known stock. He put in $2,000. Within three weeks it had dropped 70%. Later he learned the video was part of a "pump and dump," where promoters hype a stock, then sell their shares to the people who bought on the hype. Brian took what was left, opened an IRA, and started investing $150 a month in a low-cost index fund he researched using the seven checks above. "The boring plan is the one that actually builds something."

Right Side of Money Principle

Before you invest a dollar, invest an hour. Your first investment is knowledge, and it pays for the rest of your life.
