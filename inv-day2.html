Day 2 | Lesson 1

Stocks, Bonds, and Cash Investments

Almost every investment you'll ever hear about is built from these three building blocks.

Investing can sound complicated because of all the names and products. But nearly everything comes down to three basic building blocks: stocks, bonds, and cash investments. Understand these and the rest of the investing world starts to make sense.

Stocks: Owning a Piece of a Company

A share of stock is a small slice of ownership in a company. If the company grows and becomes more profitable, your slice can become more valuable. Some companies also share profits with owners through dividends, regular cash payments per share.

  • How you make money: The share price rises, dividends, or both.
  • How you lose money: The share price falls. A single company can struggle or even go bankrupt, and its stock can fall to zero.
  • Role in a portfolio: Long-term growth.

Bonds: Lending Your Money

A bond is a loan. You lend money to a government or company, and they agree to pay you interest and return your original amount, the principal, on a set date.

  • Treasury bonds are issued by the U.S. government and are considered among the safest.
  • Municipal bonds are issued by states and cities. Interest may be tax-advantaged.
  • Corporate bonds are issued by companies. They usually pay more interest than Treasuries but carry more risk.

One key rule: when interest rates rise, existing bond prices generally fall, and when rates fall, bond prices generally rise. That's because new bonds start paying the new rate, which makes older bonds more or less attractive.

Cash Investments: Safety and Access

  • High-yield savings accounts: Bank deposits, generally FDIC insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.
  • Certificates of Deposit (CDs): You agree to leave money for a set term in exchange for a set rate. Early withdrawal usually has a penalty.
  • Money market funds: Invest in short-term, high-quality debt. They're low risk but, unlike bank deposits, are not FDIC insured.
  • Treasury bills: Short-term U.S. government debt maturing in a year or less.
Building BlockWhat You OwnMain RoleMain Risk
StocksOwnership in a companyGrowthPrice swings and company failure
BondsA loan to a government or companyIncome and stabilityRising rates, borrower default
Cash investmentsDeposits and very short-term debtSafety and accessInflation eating buying power

Right Side of Money Principle

Stocks build wealth. Bonds steady the ride. Cash keeps you from being forced to sell. Each block has a job.

Day 2 | Lesson 2

Funds, ETFs, REITs, and Diversification

You don't have to pick winners. You can own the whole field.

Picking individual stocks is hard, even for professionals. The good news: most everyday investors don't need to. Funds let you own hundreds or thousands of investments in a single purchase.

Mutual Funds

A mutual fund pools money from many investors to buy a collection of stocks, bonds, or both. You own shares of the fund. Mutual funds are priced once a day after the market closes. Some are actively managed, where a manager picks investments, and some are index funds, which simply track a market index.

Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

An ETF is also a basket of investments, but it trades on an exchange throughout the day like a stock. Many ETFs track indexes and have very low costs. Many brokerages now let you buy fractional shares, so you can start with small amounts.

Index Funds

An index fund, whether a mutual fund or ETF, is designed to match the performance of a market index, such as the S&P 500 or the total U.S. stock market. Because there's no manager picking stocks, costs are usually very low.

Target-Date Funds

A target-date fund is an all-in-one fund built around the year you expect to need the money, often your retirement year. It holds a mix of stocks and bonds and automatically becomes more conservative as that year gets closer. Many workplace retirement plans use them as the default option. Costs vary widely, so check the expense ratio.

REITs: Real Estate Without a Landlord's Phone Calls

A Real Estate Investment Trust (REIT) owns or finances income-producing real estate such as apartments, warehouses, shopping centers, or cell towers. REITs are generally required to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders, so many pay relatively high dividends. Publicly traded REITs and REIT funds let you invest in real estate with a small amount and without managing property. They can still be volatile and are sensitive to interest rates.

Diversification: The Only Free Lunch

Diversification means spreading your money across many investments so that no single failure can sink you. When one company or sector struggles, others may hold up.

A Hard Lesson in Concentration

When Enron collapsed in 2001, many of its employees had a large share of their retirement savings in Enron stock. They lost their jobs and much of their retirement at the same time. Owning too much of any single company, especially your own employer, concentrates two major risks in one place.

A single broad index fund can hold thousands of companies across every major industry. That's diversification in one purchase.

Right Side of Money Principle

You don't need to find the next big winner. Owning a broad slice of the whole market has been one of the most reliable ways for everyday people to build wealth over time.

Day 2 | Lesson 3

Markets, Indexes, and Investing Approaches

Know where investments trade, how the market is measured, and which approach fits you.

When people say "the market was up today," what do they actually mean? This lesson explains where investing happens, how the market is measured, and the main approaches people use.

Markets and Exchanges

Stocks and ETFs are bought and sold on exchanges, such as the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and Nasdaq. When you place an order through a brokerage account, the brokerage executes it on your behalf. Prices move constantly based on supply and demand, company news, economic data, interest rates, and investor emotion.

Indexes: The Market's Scoreboard

An index is a list of investments used to measure part of the market. You can't invest in an index directly, but index funds let you own what's in it.

IndexWhat It Measures
S&P 500About 500 of the largest U.S. companies, weighted by size
Dow Jones Industrial Average30 large, well-known U.S. companies
Nasdaq CompositeThousands of companies listed on the Nasdaq, heavy in technology
Total U.S. stock market indexesNearly every publicly traded U.S. company, large and small
International indexesCompanies in developed and emerging markets outside the U.S.
Bond indexesBroad collections of government and corporate bonds

Investing Approaches

Passive (Index) Investing

Buy broad, low-cost index funds and hold them for the long term. You aim to match the market, not beat it.

Why Passive Has Grown

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes regular SPIVA reports comparing actively managed funds to their benchmarks. Recent reports have found that the large majority of actively managed U.S. large-cap funds, roughly nine in ten, trailed the S&P 500 over 15-year periods. Higher fees are a big part of the reason.

Active Investing

A manager, or you, selects investments trying to beat the market. It can work, but it usually costs more and is hard to do consistently.

Buy and Hold

Invest for the long term and ride out the ups and downs instead of reacting to headlines.

Trading and Day Trading

Frequently buying and selling to profit from short-term moves. Research on day traders has consistently found that most lose money over time. A widely cited study of day traders in Brazil found that the vast majority of those who persisted for over a year lost money. This is closer to speculation than investing.

Time in the Market vs. Timing the Market

Trying to jump out before drops and back in before rises sounds smart but is extremely hard to do. Some of the market's best days have historically come very close to its worst days, often during periods of fear. Missing just a handful of those best days can meaningfully reduce long-term returns. For most people, consistent, long-term investing beats trying to predict the next move.

Right Side of Money Principle

You don't have to outsmart the market. You have to outlast it.

Day 2 | Lesson 4

Fees and Expense Ratios

A fee that looks tiny today can cost you a fortune over a lifetime.

Fees are the one part of investing you can control with certainty. You can't control what the market does next year, but you can choose what you pay. And over decades, fees compound against you the same way returns compound for you.

The Fees to Know

FeeWhat It IsWhat to Look For
Expense ratioThe yearly cost of owning a fund, as a percentage of what you have invested. It's taken out automatically.Many broad index funds charge well under 0.20%. Some actively managed funds charge 1% or more.
Sales loadsA commission some mutual funds charge when you buy (front-end) or sell (back-end).Many funds are "no-load." A 5% front-end load means $50 of every $1,000 never gets invested.
Advisory feesWhat a financial adviser charges, often a percentage of your assets each year.Know exactly what you pay and what you get for it.
Trading commissionsA fee per trade.Many major brokerages now charge $0 for online stock and ETF trades.
Account feesMaintenance, inactivity, or transfer fees.Read the fee schedule before opening an account.

What a 1% Difference Really Costs

Imagine you start with $10,000, add $200 a month for 30 years, and the investments earn a hypothetical 7% a year before fees:

Annual FeeHypothetical Ending Value
0.03%About $323,000
0.50%About $291,000
1.00%About $261,000

A 1% fee instead of 0.03% costs about $62,000 in this illustration. That money went to fees instead of to your future. And the higher-cost fund has to outperform every single year just to break even with the cheaper one.

Where to Find a Fund's Fees

Every fund publishes a fact sheet and a prospectus, available on the fund company's website or through your brokerage. Look for the expense ratio, any loads, and the fee table near the front of the prospectus. If you can't find or understand the fees, that's a reason to pause, not to buy.

Right Side of Money Principle

You can't control returns. You can control costs, and costs are a guaranteed drag every single year.
