The Ways to Own Crypto, Compared

There's more than one door in. Each one comes with different protections, costs, and responsibilities.

A few years ago, the only way to own crypto was to buy it on a crypto exchange. Today you have real choices, including options inside the same brokerage and retirement accounts you'd use for stocks. Picking the right door is one of the most important decisions in this program.

Your Main Options

OptionWho Holds ItTypical CostsKey AdvantagesKey Drawbacks
Spot crypto ETF in a brokerage account or IRAA regulated fund and its custodian. You own fund shares.An annual expense ratio. Many bitcoin ETFs charge roughly 0.15% to 0.25% a year; some charge more than 1%.Familiar accounts, brokerage protections on the fund shares, can be held in an IRA for tax advantages, simple tax formsYou can't send or spend the coins. Trades only during market hours. Limited to the coins with ETFs.
Regulated crypto exchange or investing appThe company, on your behalfTrading fees and spreads, often 0.5% to 2% or more on "simple" buysBuy many different coins, trade any time, withdraw to your own walletCounterparty risk. No FDIC or SIPC coverage for the crypto itself.
Exchange, then move to self-custodyYouExchange costs plus a network fee to withdraw, and possibly a hardware walletNo company risk once it's in your walletAll responsibility is yours. Mistakes are permanent.
Payment apps with crypto featuresThe app companyOften higher spreadsConvenient, already on your phoneSome limit transfers out. Fewer coin choices. Same counterparty risk.
Crypto ATMsYou receive coins to a wallet you provideMarkups that can run well above 10%Cash in, crypto outVery expensive and the top tool of scammers. Most people should avoid them entirely.

A Short History of Crypto ETFs

  • January 2024: U.S. regulators approved the first spot bitcoin ETFs.
  • July 2024: Spot ether ETFs began trading.
  • September 2025: The SEC approved general listing standards that made it easier for more crypto funds to launch.
  • Late 2025: Funds holding other coins, such as Solana and XRP, began trading, and some started sharing staking rewards.

Whether a crypto ETF fits you depends on your goals. If you never plan to send or spend crypto and you want it inside a retirement account, it's worth comparing. If you want to learn how crypto actually works and possibly hold your own keys, an exchange may fit better. Many people use more than one.

Real-Life Case Study: Felicia's IRA Decision

Felicia, 44, wanted a small bitcoin position for the long term. She had no interest in managing keys and already had a Roth IRA at a brokerage. She compared three bitcoin ETFs and chose one with a 0.20% expense ratio. Because it sits inside her Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals in retirement will be tax-free, and she never has to track crypto cost basis.

Her nephew Dante wanted to understand the technology and eventually run his own wallet. He opened a regulated exchange account, made small recurring buys, and moved his coins to a hardware wallet once they added up to more than he'd be comfortable losing in an exchange failure. Different goals, different doors, both done on purpose.

Right Side of Money Principle

The best way to own crypto is the one that matches why you own it. Decide the purpose first, then pick the door.

How to Vet a Crypto Platform

Ten minutes of checking can save you from the next FTX. Here's the professional checklist.

The app with the best ads isn't necessarily the safest place for your money. Before you open an account or deposit a dollar, run every platform through this checklist. It's the same thinking you used in Day 2's "Five Questions," in more detail.

The Platform Vetting Checklist

CheckWhat to Look ForWhere to Find It
1. Licensed in the U.S. and your stateRegistration with FinCEN as a money services business, and money transmitter licenses in the states where it operates. New York's BitLicense is a well-known high bar.The platform's legal or licenses page; your state financial regulator; the NMLS Consumer Access website
2. Clear custody termsLanguage saying customer crypto is held separately for your benefit and isn't the company's propertyThe user agreement or terms of service
3. Proof of reservesRegular reports, verified by an outside firm, showing the company holds what customers ownThe platform's transparency page
4. Transparent feesTrading fees, spreads, and withdrawal fees clearly published before you buyThe fee schedule
5. Withdrawals allowedYou can move your crypto to your own wallet at any timeHelp center and terms
6. Security featuresAuthenticator or security-key login, withdrawal allowlists, and alertsSecurity settings page
7. Track recordYears of operation, how it handled past crashes, and any enforcement actionsNews searches and regulators' websites
8. Real supportA clear way to reach support through the app or official siteThe app itself, not search results

Instant Disqualifiers

  • Not licensed to serve customers in the U.S. or your state
  • Guaranteed returns, or yields that seem too good to be true
  • You found it through a stranger, a dating app, or a social media message
  • Withdrawals are delayed, limited, or require extra "fees"
  • The terms say deposited crypto becomes the company's property
  • Pressure to deposit quickly to get a "bonus"

A Word on "Insured"

Some platforms advertise insurance. Read carefully. It often covers the platform's own losses from certain hacks, or applies only to cash held at partner banks. It usually doesn't cover your crypto if prices fall, if you're scammed, if your account is taken over, or if the company fails.

Right Side of Money Principle

You're not choosing an app. You're choosing who you trust with your money. Check them like you would anyone else you'd hand your savings to.

How to Buy: Orders, Fees, and Your First Purchase

The difference between a careless buy and a smart one can be several percent, every single time.

Buying crypto looks simple: tap Buy, enter an amount, done. But the "simple" button is often the most expensive way to do it. Here's what you're actually paying and how to pay less.

The Costs of Buying

  • Trading fee: A stated percentage or flat fee per trade.
  • Spread: The hidden cost. The gap between the price you pay and the actual market price. "No fee" apps often make their money here.
  • Payment method fee: Debit and credit card purchases usually cost more than bank transfers.
  • Network and withdrawal fees: Charged when you move crypto to another wallet.

Dollar-Cost Averaging: Buying on a Schedule

Instead of trying to guess the right day to buy, many long-term investors buy a fixed dollar amount on a schedule, like every payday. In a market this volatile, it removes the emotional decision from each purchase. Try it with a hypothetical price path:

Your First Purchase, Step by Step

If and when you decide to buy, here's a careful way to do it. Buying is optional. You can finish this program without owning any crypto.

  1. Finish your security setup from Day 3 before depositing anything.
  2. Link a bank account rather than using a credit card. Never buy crypto with borrowed money.
  3. Start with a practice amount, small enough that a total loss wouldn't matter, to learn how everything works.
  4. Compare the total cost on the simple buy screen and the advanced trade screen before confirming.
  5. Write down the date, amount, price, and fees for your records.
  6. If you plan to self-custody, practice a small test withdrawal to your own wallet using the Safe Send Checklist.
  7. Set up recurring buys only after you've written your Crypto Investment Policy on Day 5.

Right Side of Money Principle

Fees are the one part of investing you control completely. Every percent you don't pay stays in your family.

Crypto Taxes: What the IRS Expects

Crypto isn't invisible to the IRS, and it hasn't been for years. Here's what's taxable and how to stay clean.

The IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, not currency. That means many of the same rules that apply to selling stock or a rental property apply to crypto. Every federal tax return now asks whether you received, sold, exchanged, or otherwise disposed of a digital asset.

What's Taxable and What Isn't

Usually TaxableUsually Not Taxable
Selling crypto for dollars at a gainBuying crypto with dollars and holding it
Trading one crypto for another, including into a stablecoinMoving crypto between your own wallets or accounts
Spending crypto on goods or servicesHolding crypto while the price goes up
Being paid in crypto for work (taxed as income)Buying a crypto ETF in an IRA, where taxes follow IRA rules
Staking and mining rewards (generally income when received)

That second row in the left column surprises many people: trading bitcoin for another coin is a sale. You owe tax on any gain at that moment, even though you never received dollars.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term

  • Held one year or less: Taxed at your ordinary income rate, from 10% to 37%.
  • Held more than one year: Long-term rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%. For 2026, the 0% rate applies to taxable income up to $49,450 for single filers and $98,900 for married couples filing jointly.
  • Losses can offset gains, plus up to $3,000 a year of ordinary income, with any extra carried forward to future years.

The New Reporting Rules

  • Form 1099-DA: Crypto brokers now report your sales to the IRS on a new form, similar to the 1099-B you get for stocks. Reporting of sale proceeds began with sales in 2025.
  • Cost basis reporting starts with 2026 sales of crypto bought on or after January 1, 2026, in a custodial account. For older purchases or coins moved in from elsewhere, proving what you paid is still your job.
  • The wash sale rule: As of 2026, the rule that blocks claiming a loss on stock you rebuy within 30 days generally doesn't apply to crypto held directly. Whether it applies to spot crypto ETFs isn't settled. Many are structured as trusts that the IRS may look through to the coins inside, while others may be treated like securities. Ask a tax professional before selling an ETF at a loss and buying it back within 30 days. Congress has also considered changing the rules for crypto, so check each year.

Keep Records Like a Pro

For every buy, sell, trade, and transfer, keep: the date, the coin and amount, the price in dollars, the fees, and the transaction ID. Download your platform's transaction history at least once a year, and keep it even if the platform shuts down. Crypto tax software can help, and a tax professional who knows crypto is worth it once you have more than a handful of transactions.

Real-Life Case Study: Terrence and the Surprise Bill

Terrence bought $3,000 of bitcoin in 2024. In 2025, after it rose, he traded all of it for a smaller coin he'd heard about, then traded that into a stablecoin when it dropped. He never "cashed out," so he assumed he owed nothing. His exchange's 1099-DA listed the proceeds from both trades. Once he worked out what he'd paid, he found a short-term gain of about $2,600 on the first one.

Because he hadn't kept records, he spent a weekend rebuilding his history from screenshots. Now he exports his transactions every quarter and keeps a simple spreadsheet.

Right Side of Money Principle

Every trade is a record. Keep the records, report honestly, and let time work in your favor: holding more than a year can cut your tax rate dramatically.
