Lesson 3.1

Guessing vs. Knowing Your Numbers

You can't manage what you can't measure, and you can't build on a number you made up.

Ask most people how much they spend in a month and they'll give you a number with total confidence. Then ask them to pull their bank and credit card statements and add it up. The real number is almost always higher, often by hundreds of dollars, sometimes by more than a thousand.

This isn't dishonesty. It's how human brains work. We remember the big, planned expenses (rent, car note) and forget the small, frequent ones (the $6 here, the $14 there, the auto-renewals). We're also naturally optimistic about ourselves. Psychologists call it optimism bias, and it shows up in money as "I don't spend that much on food" when the statements say otherwise.

The Core Truth

A guess feels like knowledge until reality sends the bill. Every financial plan built on guessed numbers is a plan built on sand. The shift from guessing to knowing is the single most important operational upgrade you can make. It turns anxiety into information and information into decisions.

How Far Off Are Our Guesses?

A widely cited 2022 consumer survey on subscription spending found that people estimated they spent less than $100 a month on subscriptions, while their actual spending was more than double that, a gap of over $100 a month. Subscriptions are designed to be forgotten. The same pattern shows up with dining out, convenience purchases, and small transfers. What we remember and what we spend are two different numbers.

The RSM Core 8: The Numbers Every Builder Knows

You don't need to become an accountant. You need to know eight numbers cold. When you know these, you can answer almost any financial question about your life with confidence.

1. Monthly Take-Home Income

What actually hits your bank account after taxes, insurance, and retirement deductions. Not your salary. Not your hourly rate times 40. The deposit. If your income varies (gig work, commission, overtime, tips), look at the last 6 to 12 months and use your lowest typical month as your baseline. Plan on the floor, and treat anything above it as a bonus with a job already assigned.

2. Fixed Essential Expenses

Bills that are the same (or nearly the same) every month and that you must pay: housing, car payment, insurance, utilities, phone, childcare, minimum debt payments.

3. Variable Spending (3-Month Average)

Everything that changes month to month: groceries, gas, dining out, shopping, entertainment, personal care. Use a three-month average from your actual statements, not what you think it is. One month can be misleading. Three months shows the pattern.

4. Total Debt, Minimum Payments, and Interest Rates

Every debt you owe, listed with the current balance, the minimum payment, and the interest rate (APR). Most people know roughly what they owe. Far fewer know their interest rates, and the rates are what determine which debt is hurting you most.

5. Monthly Interest Cost

How much you pay each month just for the privilege of owing money. This number wakes people up.

Monthly Interest Cost = Balance x APR / 12 (repeat for each debt and add them together)

Example: $8,400 on a credit card at 22.9% APR costs about $160 a month in interest. That's $160 that buys nothing and builds nothing.

6. Savings Rate

The percentage of your take-home income that goes to savings and investments each month.

Savings Rate = Monthly Amount Saved and Invested / Monthly Take-Home Income x 100

Many financial educators suggest aiming for at least 10% to 20%. If you're at zero, don't panic. Getting to 1% is the first win. The direction matters more than the starting point.

7. Emergency Runway

How many months you could cover your essential expenses if your income stopped today.

Emergency Runway (months) = Liquid Emergency Savings / Monthly Essential Expenses

A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses. Most people start with a first milestone of $1,000 to $2,000, then build toward one month, then three.

8. Net Worth

Everything you own minus everything you owe. It's the single scoreboard for your entire financial life.

Net Worth = Total Assets (cash, retirement, investments, home equity, vehicle value) - Total Liabilities (all debts)

If your net worth is negative, you're in good company. Many people start there, especially after student loans or medical debt. A negative net worth is a starting line, not a verdict. What matters is that it moves up every quarter.

Two Ratios That Tell the Truth Fast

Once you have the Core 8, two quick ratios show you where pressure is coming from:

  • Housing Ratio: Housing cost divided by income. A common guideline is to keep housing at or below about 30% of gross income. When it's much higher, almost every other category gets squeezed.
  • Debt Payment Ratio: Total monthly debt payments (car, credit cards, student loans, personal loans) divided by take-home income. The higher this is, the less room you have to save, invest, or absorb a surprise. Lenders use a similar measure, debt-to-income, when deciding whether to approve you for a mortgage.
Real-Life Case Study: The Johnson Household: Guessing vs. Knowing
What They Guessed

Kevin and Monique Johnson both work. When asked, they said, "We bring home about $5,800 a month and spend maybe $4,800. We should be saving around $1,000, but somehow we never do." They couldn't understand where the $1,000 went.

What the Statements Showed
CategoryMonthly Amount
Take-home income$5,800
Fixed essentials (rent $1,750, two car payments $520, car insurance $210, utilities $260, phones $140, childcare $600)$3,480
Variable spending, 3-month average (groceries $750, gas $280, eating out $420, subscriptions $95, shopping and miscellaneous $500)$2,045
Minimum payments (credit card $210, student loan $180)$390
Total outflow$5,915
Monthly result-$115
The Core 8 Revealed

They weren't missing $1,000. They were $115 short every month, and the gap was being quietly covered by the credit card, which is why the balance kept rising. Their credit card ($8,400 at 22.9%) and student loan ($19,000 at 5.5%) were costing about $247 a month in interest alone. Their savings rate was 0%. Their emergency savings of $900 against $4,900 in monthly essentials gave them a runway of about five days. Their net worth was roughly negative $16,600.

What Changed

Kevin said, "Honestly, seeing negative $115 was a relief. At least now I know what we're dealing with." Knowing the real numbers let them make real decisions: they cut eating out to $220, canceled $60 in subscriptions, and shopped their car insurance, saving $45. That turned -$115 into +$190 a month, which went straight to the credit card. For the first time in years, the balance started going down.

How to Get Your Real Numbers This Week

  1. Gather 90 days of statements from every checking account, savings account, and credit card. Download them or print them.
  2. Sort every transaction into: income, fixed essentials, variable spending, debt payments, and savings.
  3. Total each category and divide by three for monthly averages.
  4. List every debt with balance, minimum payment, and APR. Log in to each account if you don't know the APR. It's on your statement.
  5. Calculate your Core 8. Write them on one page. Date it. This is your financial baseline.

Lesson 3.2

Facing Your Financial Reality Without Panic

Panic sees everything at once. Strategy handles one thing at a time.

You've gathered your numbers. Maybe you've already seen things that made your stomach drop. A balance higher than you thought. A collection notice you forgot about. A negative net worth. A runway measured in days, not months.

This is the moment where many people spiral. The numbers become proof of every fear they've ever had, and they go right back to avoidance. This lesson exists to make sure that doesn't happen to you. You're going to learn how to look at the hardest numbers of your life calmly, sort them, and act.

The Core Truth

Panic is a physical state, not a financial strategy. When your body is in panic, the thinking part of your brain gets less of the resources it needs to plan. You can't solve a money problem from inside a panic response. Calm your body first, then work your numbers.

Why Your Body Panics Over Numbers

Your nervous system doesn't know the difference between a physical threat and a financial one. When you see a scary number, it can trigger the same stress response as danger: racing heart, shallow breathing, tunnel vision. Stress hormones prepare you to fight or run, not to calmly compare interest rates.

That's why the first step in facing your reality isn't financial. It's physical.

Box Breathing: Reset Before You Review

Box breathing is a simple, controlled breathing technique used in military and first-responder training to stay steady under stress. In the Marine Corps, you learn quickly that the person who stays calm makes the better decision. Use it before every financial review:

  1. Breathe in through your nose for a count of 4.
  2. Hold for a count of 4.
  3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth for a count of 4.
  4. Hold for a count of 4.
  5. Repeat four to six times.

This slows your breathing and signals your body to come down out of alarm. It takes about one minute. It can change the next hour.

The RSM Calm Review Protocol

This is the step-by-step process for facing your full financial reality in a single sitting without drowning in it.

Step 1: Prepare the Space

Choose a time when you're rested and fed (remember HALT from Module 1). Sit somewhere quiet. Have water, your Core 8 page, your statements, a notebook, and a highlighter. Do box breathing before you start.

Step 2: Gather Everything in One Place

List every account, bill, and debt on one sheet. Include anything you've been avoiding. It's all going on the table. Out of sight has been out of control.

Step 3: Sort Into Three Colors

ColorMeaningExamples
GreenCurrent and stable. No action needed this month.Paid-up utilities, on-time car payment, active savings account
YellowNeeds attention soon. Not urgent, but will become urgent if ignored.High-interest credit card being paid on time, subscription you meant to cancel, insurance you've never shopped
RedUrgent. Past due, in collections, or threatening essentials.Rent behind, utility shutoff notice, car payment 30 days late, collections letter, unfiled taxes

Step 4: Triage Red Items With Essentials First

When money is tight and several things are red, protect the essentials first. Many financial counselors call these "the four walls": shelter, food, utilities, and transportation to work. Keep a roof over your head, food on the table, the lights on, and a way to get to the job that pays for everything else. Only after those are secure do you move to other obligations.

This doesn't mean ignoring the other red items. It means sequencing them. A collection agency calling about an old credit card doesn't outrank keeping your family housed.

Step 5: One Next Action Per Red Item

For each red item, write one specific next action and a date. Not a full solution. One action.

  • Rent behind: "Call landlord Tuesday to propose a catch-up plan and ask about local rental assistance programs."
  • Utility notice: "Call the utility Monday and ask about payment arrangements and hardship programs."
  • Credit card 30 days late: "Call the issuer Wednesday, make a payment, and ask about a hardship program or fee waiver."

Step 6: Close With a Win

End the session by writing down what you accomplished. "I looked at everything. I have a list. I have next steps." That's a massive win. Most people never get there.

What to Know About Calling Creditors

Many people avoid calling creditors because they expect to be yelled at. The reality is usually different. Lenders generally prefer getting paid over time to sending an account to collections, and many have hardship programs that may temporarily lower interest rates, reduce payments, or waive fees. You won't know unless you ask. Call before you miss a payment whenever possible. Your options are usually best before an account goes delinquent.

When you call: be honest, be specific about what you can pay, ask directly "Do you have a hardship program?", and write down the name of the person you spoke with, the date, and what was agreed.

Real-Life Case Study: Carlos and Maria, Married With Two Kids
The Situation

Carlos, a warehouse lead, and Maria, a medical assistant, had been avoiding a full financial review for two years. When they finally did a Calm Review, the total was $41,000 in debt: two credit cards ($11,200 and $6,300), a car loan ($14,500), medical bills ($3,800, one in collections), a personal loan ($4,200), and $1,000 owed to Maria's sister. Maria cried when she saw the total. Carlos wanted to close the notebook.

The Calm Review

They stopped and did box breathing together. Then they sorted. Green: car loan (current), rent (current), utilities (current). Yellow: both credit cards (current but high interest), the personal loan, and the family loan. Red: one medical bill in collections ($1,400) and a second medical bill 60 days past due ($2,400).

The Actions

Red item one: Maria called the hospital billing office about the past-due bill and asked about financial assistance. Many hospitals, especially nonprofit hospitals, are required to have financial assistance policies. Their bill qualified for a partial reduction and a no-interest payment plan. Red item two: they requested written validation of the collections debt before paying anything and then negotiated a payment plan.

The Shift

Carlos said, "Forty-one thousand felt like a wall. Two red items felt like a Tuesday." The total didn't change that night. Their ability to act did. Within a year they had eliminated both medical bills and the family loan and were attacking the smaller credit card.

Your Action Step: Complete Your Calm Review

  1. Schedule a 60 to 90 minute block this week for your Calm Review.
  2. Do box breathing before you begin.
  3. Follow all six steps. Sort every account and bill into Green, Yellow, or Red.
  4. Write one next action and a date for every Red item.
  5. Complete at least one Red action within 72 hours.

Lesson 3.3

Identifying Your Top Money Leaks

Big goals don't usually die from big expenses. They bleed out from small, forgotten ones.

Imagine trying to fill a bathtub with the drain half open. You can turn the faucet up as high as it goes (work more hours, get a raise, start a side hustle) and the water level barely rises. Most people respond by turning the faucet higher. Builders find the drain.

A money leak is any recurring outflow that doesn't match your values or your goals, and usually one you don't notice. Leaks are dangerous precisely because each one feels small. Nobody's retirement was ruined by one $14.99 charge. But leaks don't come one at a time. They come in groups, and they come every single month.

The Core Truth

The money you need for your goals is often already flowing through your hands. It's just leaking out before it gets anywhere. Plugging leaks is the fastest raise you'll ever give yourself, because every dollar you stop leaking is a dollar you keep, with no extra hours and no extra taxes.

The Seven Types of Money Leaks

Leak TypeWhat It Looks LikeWhy It Hides
Subscription LeaksStreaming services, apps, memberships, software, boxes, premium upgradesAuto-renew and small amounts are designed to be forgotten
Convenience LeaksDelivery apps, rideshare, convenience store runs, grab-and-go mealsEach purchase feels like it saves time or effort
Interest LeaksCredit card interest, payday loans, high-rate car loans, buy now pay later late feesInterest is buried inside the minimum payment
Fee LeaksOverdraft fees, ATM fees, late fees, monthly maintenance fees, paper statement feesThey show up as small line items that look like bank noise
Never-Shopped LeaksCar and home insurance, phone plan, internet, cable you never compared or renegotiatedSet it and forget it means you may be overpaying for years
Lifestyle Creep LeaksUpgrades that became "normal": premium gas, name brands, bigger phone plan, pricier haircutEach upgrade felt deserved and then became permanent
Obligation LeaksRegular unplanned money to family or friends, loans that never come back, covering others' billsGuilt and love make them hard to question

The Power of Annualizing

Small numbers hide. Annual numbers wake you up. Always multiply a monthly leak by 12 before deciding if it's "no big deal."

Monthly LeakPer YearOver 10 Years (if invested at a hypothetical 7% average annual return)
$100$1,200About $17,300
$150$1,800About $26,000
$300$3,600About $51,900
$612$7,344About $105,900

Illustration only. The 7% figure is a hypothetical average annual return used to show how compounding works. Actual investment returns vary, can be negative, and aren't guaranteed.

That last column is the real cost of a leak. It's not just the money you spend. It's what that money could have become.

The RSM Leak Audit

  1. Pull 90 days of statements from every account and card. (You have these from Lesson 3.1.)
  2. Highlight every recurring charge, anything that appears monthly or more than twice in 90 days.
  3. Highlight every fee and every interest charge.
  4. Label each highlight with one of the seven leak types.
  5. Annualize it. Monthly amount times 12.
  6. Apply the Value-Per-Dollar Test: For each item, ask: "If I were signing up for this today, knowing the annual cost, would I say yes?" If the answer isn't a clear yes, it's a leak.

The Plug Plan: Five Ways to Stop a Leak

  • Cancel: End it completely. Unused subscriptions, duplicate services, memberships you don't use.
  • Reduce: Keep it, but at a lower level. Downgrade the plan, lower the frequency, set a cap.
  • Replace: Meet the same need a cheaper way. Library instead of audiobook subscription. Meal prep instead of weekday delivery.
  • Renegotiate: Call and ask. Insurance, phone, internet, and credit card rates are often negotiable, especially if you've compared competitors' prices first.
  • Redirect: This is the step everyone skips, and it's the most important. The moment you plug a leak, set up an automatic transfer of that exact amount to a goal: emergency fund, debt payoff, or investing. If you don't redirect the money, it will quietly get absorbed somewhere else and you'll have nothing to show for your effort.

A plugged leak without a redirect is just a leak that moved.

Real-Life Case Study: Tasha, Dental Hygienist
The Situation

Tasha, 31, earns good money and couldn't understand why she never had anything left. She was sure she didn't have leaks: "I don't buy expensive stuff."

What the Leak Audit Found
LeakTypeMonthly
8 subscriptions (4 streaming, 2 apps, meal kit she skipped half the time, cloud storage upgrade)Subscription$118
Food delivery, about 9 orders a monthConvenience$236

The Plug Plan

Tasha canceled five subscriptions and the gym, reduced delivery to two planned orders a month, dropped the phone protection plan, switched to a bank with no overdraft fees and a large free ATM network, and redirected everything she plugged, about $420 a month, to her credit card on autopay on top of the roughly $140 she was already paying.

The Result

Her $7,000 balance was gone in about 15 months, which also eliminated the $135 a month interest leak. She then redirected the full amount into savings and investing. Same income. No second job. She just found the drain.

Your Action Step: Complete Your Leak Audit and Plug Plan

  1. Complete the six-step Leak Audit using your 90 days of statements.
  2. Identify your top three leaks by annual cost.
  3. Assign each one a Plug Plan action: Cancel, Reduce, Replace, or Renegotiate.
  4. Take action on all three within seven days.
  5. Set up an automatic Redirect for the total amount you plugged. Name the transfer after its goal, for example "Debt Freedom" or "Emergency Fund."
