Lesson 1 of 8

How Banks Make Money From Your Money

When you deposit money, it doesn't sit in a vault with your name on it. Here's what really happens, and who profits.

Legally, the moment you deposit money in a bank, it stops being your money. It becomes the bank's money, and the bank owes you the same amount. Your deposit is a loan you've made to the bank. On the bank's books, your account is a liability, something it owes. You're a creditor of the bank.

That's why the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) exists. Created in 1933 after thousands of banks failed, it insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. Credit unions have similar coverage through the NCUA. If your bank fails, insurance covers you up to the limit. Above the limit, you're at risk.

Revenue stream 1: the spread

Banks take in deposits and pay you a small amount of interest, often close to nothing. Then they lend money out at much higher rates. The difference is called the spread, or net interest margin. It's the core of the banking business.

What the bank pays youTypical rateWhat the bank charges othersTypical rate

Rates change with the economy and vary by bank and credit score. These ranges show the general gap, not a quote.

Revenue stream 2: fees

Overdraft fees, non-sufficient funds fees, monthly maintenance fees, ATM fees, wire fees, and late fees. For years, U.S. banks collected billions of dollars a year in overdraft and insufficient funds fees alone, and those fees landed hardest on customers with the lowest balances.

Revenue stream 3: your card swipes

Every time you use a debit or credit card, the merchant pays a fee, usually a small percentage of the purchase. Part of that interchange fee goes to the bank that issued your card. Merchants often build that cost into their prices, so everyone pays it, even people paying cash.

Revenue stream 4: your interest

If you carry a credit card balance, you're one of the most profitable customers a bank can have. A $5,000 balance at 22% generates about $1,100 a year in interest for the bank. The same $5,000 in a traditional savings account at 0.5% earns you about $25.

Two sides of the same bank

Jasmine keeps $10,000 in savings at her big bank, earning 0.5%. Her bank makes her $50 a year. Her coworker Derrick carries a $10,000 credit card balance at the same bank at 24%. He pays the bank about $2,400 a year.

The bank is using money like Jasmine's to fund lending like Derrick's. It pays one customer $50 and collects $2,400 from another. That's the spread. The goal isn't to hate the bank. The goal is to stop being Derrick and start thinking like the bank.

Right Side move

  1. Pull up your last bank statement and add up every fee you paid this year. That's money you gave away for nothing.
  2. Compare your savings rate to what online high-yield savings accounts and local credit unions pay. The difference is often many times more interest for the same safety, as long as the account is FDIC or NCUA insured.
  3. If you carry card balances, you're on the paying side of the spread. Lesson 4 gives you the plan to switch sides.

Lesson 2 of 8

How Money Is Created

Most people think money is printed by the government. Most of it isn't. The majority of money in your economy is created when someone takes out a loan.

This is one of the most eye-opening facts in all of finance, and it isn't a conspiracy theory. It comes straight from central banks. In 2014, the Bank of England published a paper titled "Money creation in the modern economy." It explained that commercial banks create most of the money in the economy, in the form of bank deposits, by making new loans.

How it works, step by step

Kevin buys a car

Kevin gets approved for a $30,000 car loan. The bank doesn't go to a vault and take out someone else's savings. It does two things with a few keystrokes:

1. It records a new asset on its books: Kevin's promise to pay back $30,000 plus interest.
2. It creates a new deposit of $30,000 and sends it to the dealership.

Nobody's savings account went down. The dealership now has $30,000 in its account that didn't exist yesterday. New money was created, and it was created as debt.

When Kevin pays the loan back, the reverse happens. The money he repays cancels the debt and effectively disappears from the money supply. That's why the money supply depends so heavily on lending. When people and businesses borrow a lot, the money supply grows. When they pay down debt and stop borrowing, it can shrink.

So what limits banks?

Banks can't create unlimited money. They're limited by:

  • Demand and risk. They need borrowers who are likely to pay them back and loans that are profitable.
  • Capital requirements. Regulators require banks to hold a cushion of their own capital against their loans.
  • Interest rates set by the Fed. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so fewer people borrow and less money is created.

What about reserve requirements, the old rule that banks had to keep a percentage of deposits on hand? In March 2020, the Fed set reserve requirements to zero. They've stayed there.

Where the rest comes from

Physical cash
Printed by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing and minted by the U.S. Mint. Only a small slice, roughly a tenth, of the money supply.
Bank lending
The biggest source. New deposits are created when loans are made.
Government borrowing
The Treasury borrows by selling bonds. The money gets spent into the economy.
The Federal Reserve
Creates bank reserves electronically when it buys assets. That's quantitative easing, coming in Lesson 6.

The big idea

In our system, money and debt are two sides of the same coin. Almost every dollar in your checking account exists because someone, somewhere, borrowed it into existence. That means the system needs people to keep borrowing. Now think about how much advertising you see for loans, cards, and "buy now, pay later." It makes sense, doesn't it?

Right Side move

Every loan offer you get is an invitation to create new money for someone else's benefit. Before you accept one, ask: "Will this debt help me build something, or just help me buy something?" If it's the second one, walk away for 48 hours before you decide.

Lesson 3 of 8

How Debt Actually Works

Debt is designed so the lender gets paid first. Once you see how the math works, you'll never look at a monthly payment the same way.

Interest is the price you pay to use someone else's money. The APR (annual percentage rate) tells you that price per year. But what matters is how interest is charged and how your payments are applied.

Compound interest: the force that works both ways

On most credit cards, interest compounds. You get charged interest on your balance, and if you don't pay it off, next month you're charged interest on the interest. When you save or invest, compounding works for you. When you owe, it works against you.

Amortization: why your balance barely moves

On an installment loan like a mortgage or car loan, your payment stays the same each month. But the split between interest and principal changes. Early on, most of each payment goes to interest. The lender gets paid first.

A $300,000 mortgage at 7% for 30 years

Monthly payment (principal and interest): $1,995.91.

In the very first month, $1,750 of that payment is interest. Only about $246 goes toward the house.

Over 30 years, the borrower pays about $418,500 in interest. That's more than the price of the house itself.

The minimum payment trap

Credit card minimum payments are set low on purpose. Try this calculator with your own numbers.

With the default numbers, a $5,000 balance at 22% paid at $125 a month takes over six years to clear and costs about $4,100 in interest. In the first year alone, only about $440 of the $1,500 you pay actually reduces the balance.

Your credit score: your price tag as a borrower

Your credit score decides the interest rate you're offered. A higher score can save you tens of thousands of dollars over a lifetime. FICO, the most widely used score, is built from five parts:

FactorWeightWhat it means for you

Length of credit history15%Older accounts help. Think twice before closing your oldest card.

Productive debt vs. consumer debt

Not all debt is the same. Wealthy people and businesses use debt all the time. The difference is what the debt buys.

Productive debt
Borrowing to buy something that produces income or grows in value more than the debt costs: a rental property, a business, sometimes an education with a clear payoff.
Consumer debt
Borrowing to buy things that lose value or get used up: clothes, vacations, dinners, most cars. You pay interest on a shrinking asset.

Even productive debt carries risk. Borrowing to invest magnifies gains and losses. That's why understanding comes before borrowing.

Right Side move

Pull up your highest-interest credit card. Enter the balance, the APR, and your usual payment into the Minimum Payment Trap calculator above. Write down the total interest number and tape it somewhere you'll see it every day. That number is what the bank plans to collect from you.

Lesson 4 of 8

Debt Strategy

Knowing how debt works is step one. Beating it takes a plan. Here's a proven one you can start this week.

Step 1: Stop the bleeding

You can't bail out a boat while water's still pouring in. Stop adding new consumer debt. Take the cards out of your wallet and your phone's saved payments if you need to. Pay cash or debit while you're getting out.

Step 2: Build a starter cushion

Set aside a small emergency fund first, even $500 to $1,000. Without it, the next flat tire goes right back on a card and you lose momentum. Once your high-interest debt is gone, you'll build this up to three to six months of expenses.

Step 3: List everything

Write down every debt: the name, balance, interest rate, and minimum payment. Most people have never seen all their debt in one place. It's uncomfortable. It's also the moment you take control back.

Step 4: Pick your method

Debt AvalancheDebt Snowball
How it worksPay minimums on everything. Put every extra dollar on the debt with the highest interest rate.Pay minimums on everything. Put every extra dollar on the debt with the smallest balance.
StrengthSaves the most money on interest. Mathematically the fastest.Quick wins build motivation. You see accounts close sooner.
Best forPeople driven by numbers who can stay patient.People who need to see progress to keep going.

With both methods, when one debt is paid off, you roll its payment into the next one. Your payment power grows each time, like a snowball rolling downhill. Research from Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Management found that people who focused on closing out whole accounts were more likely to eliminate their debt overall. The best method is the one you'll actually stick with.

Step 5: Lower your interest rates

  • Call and ask. A phone call asking for a lower APR works more often than people think, especially with a good payment history.
  • Balance transfer cards can offer 0% for a set period. Watch for transfer fees, usually 3% to 5%, and have a plan to pay it off before the promo ends.
  • Consolidation loans from a credit union can replace several high-rate debts with one lower-rate payment. Only do this if you've stopped adding new debt.
  • Avoid "debt relief" pitches that promise to erase debt for an upfront fee. The Federal Trade Commission regularly warns about these.

Step 6: Automate and accelerate

Set up automatic minimum payments so you never miss one. Put tax refunds, bonuses, and side income toward your target debt. Every dollar you send early is interest you'll never pay.

Right Side move

Put your real debts into the planner above. Choose your method, write down your target debt, and set up an automatic extra payment toward it this week, even if it's just $25. Then write your debt-free date on a calendar. A goal with a date on it becomes a plan.

Lesson 5 of 8

Inflation

You don't need an economics degree to understand inflation. You just need to remember what a gallon of gas used to cost.

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. Another way to say it: it's the loss of your money's purchasing power. When inflation is 4%, something that cost $100 last year costs about $104 now. Your $100 bill didn't change. What it can buy did.

How it's measured

The most common measure is the Consumer Price Index (CPI), published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks prices of a basket of goods and services households buy: food, rent, gas, medical care, clothing, and more. The Fed also watches a related measure called PCE. Its target is 2% inflation a year.

What causes it

Demand-pull
Too much money chasing too few goods. Spending grows faster than what the economy can produce.
Cost-push
Costs to make things rise, like energy, wages, or supply shortages, and businesses pass them on.
Monetary expansion
The money supply grows faster than the economy over time. Many economists see this as the long-run driver.
~97%How much purchasing power the dollar has lost since the Fed was created in 1913, based on CPI
9.1%Inflation in June 2022, the highest since 1981
24 yrsHow long it takes prices to double at 3% inflation, by the Rule of 72

The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by an interest or inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes something to double. At 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years. At 6%, they double in about 12 years. The same rule works for growth: money growing at 8% doubles in about 9 years.

Real vs. nominal

If your savings account pays 4% and inflation is 3%, your real return is about 1%. If your savings pays 0.5% and inflation is 3%, your real return is about negative 2.5%. You're losing buying power every year, even though your balance is going up. The same goes for raises. A 3% raise during 5% inflation is a pay cut in disguise.

Who inflation hurts and who it helps

  • Hurts: savers holding cash, people on fixed incomes, workers whose raises lag behind prices, and lenders at fixed rates.
  • Helps: borrowers with fixed-rate debt (they repay with cheaper dollars), and owners of assets that tend to rise with prices, like real estate, businesses, and stocks over long periods.
  • Deflation, falling prices, sounds good but can be dangerous. People delay purchases, businesses cut jobs, and debts get harder to repay. That's why the Fed targets low, positive inflation instead of zero.

Right Side move

Look at how much you're holding in cash and savings. Plug it into the calculator above with 3% inflation and 10 years. That gap is what doing nothing costs you. Keep your emergency fund safe and accessible, but know that money you won't need for years should be positioned to outgrow inflation. You'll learn how on Day 3.

Lesson 6 of 8

Quantitative Easing

It sounds technical. It's actually simple: the central bank creates new money and uses it to buy financial assets. Here's how it's changed your world.

Normally, the Fed steers the economy by raising or lowering short-term interest rates. But in 2008, it cut rates to nearly zero and the economy was still in free fall. It needed another tool. That tool was quantitative easing, or QE.

How QE works

  1. The Fed announces it will buy large amounts of assets, mainly U.S. Treasury bonds and mortgage-backed securities.
  2. It buys them from banks and other financial institutions.
  3. It pays by creating new bank reserves electronically. No printing press. It adds money to the sellers' accounts that didn't exist before.
  4. That pushes bond prices up and long-term interest rates down, making it cheaper to borrow for homes, businesses, and the government.

The rounds

PeriodWhat happened
2008 to 2014Three rounds of QE after the financial crisis. The Fed's balance sheet grew from under $1 trillion to about $4.5 trillion.
2017 to 2019The Fed slowly shrank its holdings. This is called quantitative tightening, or QT.
March 2020The Fed pledged to buy assets "in the amounts needed." Its balance sheet roughly doubled, to about $9 trillion by 2022.
2022 onwardWith inflation high, the Fed reversed course with QT, letting bonds mature without replacing them.

What QE did

Supporters say: QE helped prevent a deeper depression in 2008 and a financial collapse in 2020. Lower rates helped people refinance, businesses survive, and hiring recover.

Critics say: QE inflated the prices of stocks, bonds, and homes, which mostly benefited people who already owned them. It encouraged governments and companies to pile on cheap debt. And when combined with pandemic spending, it helped fuel the 2021 to 2022 inflation.

Both can be true at the same time. That's the point. Policy that "saves the system" doesn't help everyone in the system equally.

What this meant for regular families

Between 2020 and 2022, home prices across the U.S. rose sharply as mortgage rates hit record lows. Families who already owned homes saw their wealth jump. Families trying to buy their first home watched prices run away from them, and then got hit with much higher mortgage rates in 2022 when the Fed reversed course. Same policy. Opposite results. The difference was ownership.

Right Side move

When you hear that the Fed is "easing" or "cutting," think: asset prices may rise and borrowing gets cheaper. When you hear "tightening" or "hiking," think: borrowing gets expensive and asset prices may cool. You don't need to predict it. You just need to stop being surprised by it.

Lesson 7 of 8

The "Hidden Tax" Concept

There's a tax you've paid your whole life. It never appears on a pay stub, no one ever voted for it, and it takes from savers first.

When the government wants more money, it has three basic options: raise taxes, borrow, or let new money be created. Raising taxes is unpopular and takes a vote. Borrowing adds to the debt. But when new money enters the system and prices rise, the value of every existing dollar goes down. Economists call this effect the inflation tax.

"By a continuing process of inflation, governments can confiscate, secretly and unobserved, an important part of the wealth of their citizens."John Maynard Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace, 1919

Keynes was one of the most influential economists of the 20th century, and he wasn't a critic of government spending. That's what makes his warning so powerful.

How the hidden tax reaches you

1. It shrinks your savings

If you hold $10,000 in cash and inflation runs 4% for a year, you've lost about $400 of buying power. Nobody sent you a bill. It just quietly disappeared.

2. It helps big borrowers

The U.S. government is the largest borrower in the world. Inflation makes its debts easier to repay because it pays back with dollars worth less than the ones it borrowed. The cost is shifted to people holding those dollars.

3. It taxes you on "gains" that aren't real

If you earn 5% interest while inflation is 4%, your real gain is about 1%. But you owe income tax on the full 5%. After taxes, you may actually be losing ground.

4. Thresholds that never move

Federal income tax brackets are adjusted for inflation each year. But several important tax thresholds are not. As incomes rise with inflation, more people cross them every year without any new law being passed.

ThresholdSet inAdjusted for inflation?
Income level where Social Security benefits start to be taxed ($25,000 single / $32,000 married)1983No
Net Investment Income Tax threshold ($200,000 single / $250,000 married)2013No
Capital loss you can deduct against regular income each year ($3,000)1978No

When Social Security's thresholds were set in 1983, most retirees paid no tax on their benefits. Today, because the thresholds never moved, a large share of retirees do. No one voted for that increase. Inflation did it.

Right Side move

Start thinking in "real" numbers. When you get a raise, subtract inflation. When you see a savings rate, subtract inflation. When you plan for retirement, assume prices will keep climbing. People on the right side of money measure what their money can buy, not just how big the number looks.

Lesson 8 of 8

Follow the Money

New money doesn't land on everyone at the same time. Who gets it first, and who gets it last, explains a lot about why the gap between the haves and have-nots keeps growing.

In the early 1700s, an Irish-French banker named Richard Cantillon noticed something. When new money enters an economy, it doesn't raise all prices at once. It flows from one person to the next. The people who receive new money first can spend or invest it before prices rise. The people who receive it last pay the higher prices before their income catches up. This is known today as the Cantillon effect.

The order money flows

WhoHow they get new moneyResult
First: government and big financial institutionsGovernment borrowing and spending. Fed asset purchases from banks. Cheap loans.Spend or invest at today's prices.
Next: asset ownersLower rates and more money push up prices of stocks, bonds, real estate, and businesses.Their wealth grows, often before prices rise elsewhere.
Then: businessesMore spending and credit flow through the economy.Raise prices as demand grows.
Last: wage earners, savers, fixed incomesRaises come slowly, often once a year, and have to be negotiated.Pay higher prices first, get compensated later, or never.

What the numbers show

According to the Federal Reserve's own data, the wealthiest 10% of American households own roughly 90% of all stocks. When new money pushes stock prices up, most of that gain goes to them. Meanwhile, during the 2021 to 2022 inflation, prices rose faster than wages for many workers, so paychecks bought less even as they grew. That's the Cantillon effect in real life.

Three questions to ask about anything

Whenever you hear about a new policy, a new product, or a new "opportunity," ask three questions:

  1. Who gets the money first?
  2. Who pays for it?
  3. Where does the money end up?

Use them on a bailout, a stimulus package, a "free" app, a car dealership's financing offer, or a friend's business pitch. The answers will tell you more than any headline.

Follow the money in your own life

Now turn those questions inward. Look at your last month of spending and ask: where did my money end up? For most people, it ends up with landlords, lenders, car companies, phone companies, and subscription services, all of them owners. The goal of Day 3 is to start sending some of that money to the one owner that matters most: you.

Right Side move

Go through last month's bank statement with a highlighter. Mark every payment that went to a company you could own a piece of: your phone carrier, your grocery chain, your streaming services. You're already their customer. On Day 3, you'll learn how regular people become owners too.

Day 2 · Right Side of Money Takeaway

The system isn't broken. It's working exactly as designed.

Today you saw the machinery behind every paycheck, loan, and price tag. Here's what to carry forward.

Money is created as debt, flows first to those closest to it, and slowly loses value in the hands of those who only hold it.

What you now know

  1. Your deposit is a loan to the bank. Banks profit from the spread, from fees, from card swipes, and most of all from interest on consumer debt.
  2. Most money is created when loans are made. Money and debt are two sides of the same coin.
  3. Debt is structured so the lender gets paid first. Minimum payments and amortization keep you paying longer.
  4. You can beat debt with a plan: stop adding, build a cushion, list it all, pick avalanche or snowball, lower your rates, and automate.
  5. Inflation steadily shrinks purchasing power, and quantitative easing creates new money that lifts asset prices first.
  6. The hidden tax and the Cantillon effect shift wealth away from savers and wage earners toward borrowers and owners.

The Right Side of Money difference

People on the wrong side of money pay the spread, hold cash that shrinks, and get new money last. People on the right side understand the rules and use them: they eliminate expensive debt, measure everything in real terms, and move toward ownership. You can't change how the system works. You can change where you stand in it.
