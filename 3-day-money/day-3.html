Lesson 1 of 6

Past Financial Crises

Crises feel sudden. They almost never are. Once you learn the pattern, you'll see the next one coming, and you'll know how to be ready.

CrisisWhat happenedThe lesson
Panic of 1907A failed stock scheme set off bank runs. With no central bank, J.P. Morgan led a private rescue.Confidence is the foundation of banking. When it breaks, it breaks fast.
1929 Crash and Great DepressionStocks bought with borrowed money collapsed. Thousands of banks failed between 1930 and 1933. Unemployment reached about 25%.Borrowing heavily to speculate turns a drop into a disaster.
1970s StagflationHigh inflation and high unemployment at the same time. Fed Chair Paul Volcker pushed interest rates to around 20% by 1981 to break inflation.Inflation, once it takes hold, is painful to stop. Savers holding cash lost badly.
Black Monday, 1987The Dow fell about 22.6% in a single day, its largest one-day percentage drop ever.Markets can fall hard and fast, and then recover. Panic selling locks in losses.
Dot-com Bust, 2000Internet stocks soared on hype, then the Nasdaq fell nearly 78% from its peak by 2002.A good story isn't the same as a good investment. Profits matter.
2008 Global Financial CrisisRisky mortgages, heavy borrowing, and complex bets collapsed. Lehman Brothers failed on September 15, 2008. Congress created the $700 billion TARP bailout. Millions of families lost homes.Easy credit feeds bubbles. When bubbles pop, big institutions get rescued first.
COVID Crash, 2020Stocks fell about a third in weeks. The government and Fed responded with trillions in support. Markets recovered within months, then inflation followed.Rescues work fast for asset owners. The bill comes later, often as inflation.
Bank failures, 2023Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023 after depositors tried to pull out about $42 billion in a single day, many using their phones.Bank runs now move at internet speed. Know your insurance limits.

The pattern behind every crisis

  1. Easy money. Low rates and loose lending make borrowing cheap.
  2. Leverage. People and institutions borrow heavily to buy assets.
  3. Euphoria. Prices rise. Everyone believes "this time is different."
  4. A trigger. Rates rise, a big player fails, or confidence cracks.
  5. Panic. Everyone rushes for the exit at once. Prices crash.
  6. Rescue. Governments and central banks step in, usually helping the biggest institutions first.
  7. Recovery. Those who held on, or who had cash and courage to buy, come out ahead.

Two families, one crisis

In 2006, the Carters took a cash-out refinance on their home to buy a new truck and take a vacation. When prices fell in 2008 and Mr. Carter's hours were cut, they owed more than the house was worth. They lost it.

Their neighbors, the Joneses, had a fixed-rate mortgage, six months of expenses saved, and no credit card debt. They kept investing a little each month through the crash. By 2013, their retirement accounts were worth more than before the crisis.

Same street. Same crisis. Different position.

Right Side move

Crisis-proof yourself with three moves: build an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses, avoid high-interest and variable-rate debt, and keep your deposits within FDIC or NCUA insurance limits. People with cash and low debt don't just survive crises. They're positioned to take advantage of them.

Bonus Lesson

Where Did Taxes Come From?

For most of American history, there was no federal income tax. Here's how it arrived, why 1913 matters so much, and why the tax code rewards owners over workers.

Taxes are as old as governments. Ancient Egypt taxed harvests. Rome collected taxes across its empire, including an inheritance tax under Emperor Augustus. In 1215, English nobles forced King John to sign the Magna Carta, which limited his power to raise certain taxes without their consent. And the American Revolution rallied around the cry of "no taxation without representation."

The American road to the income tax

  1. 1794The Whiskey RebellionFarmers in Pennsylvania revolt against a federal tax on whiskey. President Washington sends troops.
  2. 1861The first federal income taxCreated to pay for the Civil War. It was repealed in 1872.
  3. 1895Struck downThe Supreme Court rules a new federal income tax unconstitutional in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust Co.
  4. 1913The 16th AmendmentRatified in February 1913, it gives Congress the power to tax incomes. Rates started at 1% and topped out at 7% on income over $500,000. Most Americans paid nothing.
  5. 1943Withholding beginsTo fund World War II, taxes are taken directly out of paychecks before workers ever see the money. The income tax becomes a mass tax.
  6. 1944Top rate hits 94%On the highest incomes, during the war.

Connect the dots

1913 gave America two things in the same year: the Federal Reserve, which manages the money supply, and the federal income tax, which collects from your paycheck. One shapes what your money is worth. The other decides how much of it you keep. Together, they're the two biggest forces on your finances, and both are over 110 years old.

Why the tax code favors owners

The tax code isn't just a way to collect money. It's a set of incentives. Congress uses it to encourage the behavior it wants: investing, building businesses, buying property, and creating jobs. That means the rules are different depending on how you earn your money.

How you earnHow it's generally taxed
Wages (W-2 job)Ordinary income tax rates, plus Social Security and Medicare taxes (7.65% from the employee, matched by the employer). Withheld before you're paid.
Long-term investment gains (assets held over a year)Lower capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income. No Social Security or Medicare tax. You're taxed only when you sell.
Business ownersCan deduct legitimate business expenses before calculating tax. Pay tax on profit, not revenue.
Real estate investorsCan deduct depreciation, which often lowers taxable rental income. Certain exchanges can defer tax when selling one property to buy another.

This isn't about avoiding taxes. It's about understanding that the tax code is written to reward ownership. Every tax break listed above is legal and available to anyone who owns the right kinds of assets.

Right Side move

Look at last year's tax return. Find the line that shows your total tax. Divide it by your total income. That's your effective tax rate. Then ask: how much of my income came from work, and how much came from things I own? Changing that mix is one of the most powerful moves you can make. A qualified tax professional can help you plan it.

Lesson 2 of 6

Asset Ownership

If you remember one lesson from this entire program, make it this one. The biggest dividing line in wealth isn't income. It's ownership.

An asset, in the simplest terms, is something that produces income or grows in value over time. A liability is something that costs you money to keep. By that definition, a lot of what people call assets, like a new car or a closet full of designer clothes, are really liabilities.

$396,200Median net worth of U.S. homeowners in 2022 (Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances)
$10,400Median net worth of U.S. renters in 2022, same survey
~90%Share of all U.S. stocks owned by the wealthiest 10% of households

Homeownership isn't the only reason for that gap, and owning a home comes with real costs. But the pattern is clear: people who own things that grow build wealth. People who only earn and spend usually don't.

The main types of assets

Stocks
Ownership shares in companies. Index funds let you own a small piece of hundreds of companies at once, at low cost.
Real estate
Your home, rental properties, or real estate investment trusts (REITs), which trade like stocks.
A business
From a side hustle to a full company. Often the fastest path to wealth, and the riskiest.
Bonds
Loans you make to governments or companies. Lower risk, lower returns.
Intellectual property
Books, music, courses, designs, and content that earn royalties or sales over time.
Yourself
Skills, credentials, and relationships that raise your income. Often the highest return of all.

Owners vs. lenders vs. consumers

Every dollar you have can play one of three roles:

  • Consumer: you spend it. It's gone.
  • Lender: you save it in a bank or buy bonds. You earn interest, but inflation and taxes often eat most of it.
  • Owner: you buy a piece of something productive. You share in its growth and income. Higher risk in the short run, but historically the path to long-term wealth.

How regular people start owning

  1. Get the employer match. If your job offers a 401(k) or 403(b) match, contribute at least enough to get all of it. It's part of your pay that you're leaving on the table otherwise.
  2. Open an IRA or Roth IRA. These accounts give you tax advantages for investing. Contribution limits change yearly, so check the current IRS limits.
  3. Start with broad, low-cost index funds. Instead of picking stocks, own a slice of the whole market.
  4. Automate it. Set a fixed amount to invest every payday, even $25. That's called dollar-cost averaging, and it removes the temptation to time the market.
  5. Think bigger over time: real estate, a business, or intellectual property. Build knowledge before you build risk.

Know the risks

Owning assets means values go up and down. Stocks have fallen 30%, 40%, even 50% during crises. Real estate can lose value and costs money to maintain. Businesses fail. The way owners win over time is by owning quality assets, staying diversified, having an emergency fund so they never have to sell at the bottom, and giving it time.

Right Side move

Write down every asset and every liability you have. Add up your assets, subtract your liabilities, and that's your net worth. It might be negative. That's okay. It's your starting line. Then choose one ownership step from the list above and take it within the next 7 days.

Lesson 3 of 6

Why Financial Literacy Isn't Universally Taught in Schools

You spent 12 years in school and may never have learned how a credit card works. That's not an accident, and it's not your fault. But it is your responsibility now.

Think about everything you've learned in the last two days. Most adults in America were never taught any of it. For generations, students learned algebra and chemistry but graduated without knowing how interest works, how to read a pay stub, or how to build credit.

Where things stand

30States that now require a standalone personal finance course to graduate high school (Next Gen Personal Finance, 2026)
76%Share of public high school students who'll be covered once those laws take full effect, with the class of 2031

That's real progress, and most of it happened in just the last few years. But it also means tens of millions of working adults graduated long before these requirements existed, and many states still don't guarantee a course.

Why it took so long

  1. Local control. In the U.S., states and school districts decide what's taught. There's no national requirement.
  2. Crowded schedules and testing. Schools focus on subjects that appear on standardized tests. Personal finance usually didn't.
  3. Teacher training. Many teachers were never trained in personal finance themselves.
  4. "That's the parents' job." For decades, money was seen as something families should teach at home. But families can only pass down what they know. If your parents weren't taught, they couldn't teach you.
  5. Who benefits from not knowing? Many people also point out that an uninformed public is profitable. Overdraft fees, high-interest debt, and expensive financial products earn the most from people who don't understand them. You don't have to believe anyone planned it that way to see who benefits.

Does financial education actually help?

Yes. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that after states required financial education, young people in those states had higher credit scores and lower rates of serious loan delinquency. Knowledge changes behavior.

Breaking the cycle

Financial knowledge compounds across generations the same way money does. A parent who understands credit teaches a child who never gets trapped by it. That child buys a home earlier, starts investing sooner, and passes both the knowledge and the assets down. The gap between families who know and families who don't grows with every generation.

You're the one who gets to change that for your family. Right now. Today.

Right Side move

Teach one thing you learned in this program to someone younger than you this week: a child, grandchild, niece, nephew, or coworker. Explain the Rule of 72, or how a credit card minimum payment works. Teaching locks in what you know, and it starts breaking the cycle in your family.

Lesson 4 of 6

Mindset, Awareness, and Taking Control

Knowledge shows you the path. Mindset decides whether you walk it. This is where information turns into transformation.

Everyone has a money story, a set of beliefs about money you picked up long before you had a paycheck. You heard them at the dinner table, saw them in your neighborhood, and felt them every time the lights got cut off or a bill went unpaid. Those beliefs run in the background of every financial decision you make.

Money scripts

Financial psychologist Dr. Brad Klontz and his colleagues identified four common patterns of money beliefs, which they call money scripts:

Money avoidance
"Money is bad." "Rich people are greedy." "I don't deserve it." Leads to avoiding money topics and self-sabotage.
Money worship
"More money will solve all my problems." Leads to overspending and never feeling like it's enough.
Money status
"My worth equals my net worth." Leads to buying things to look successful instead of being successful.
Money vigilance
"Always be careful and secretive with money." Can protect you, but in excess leads to anxiety and fear of investing.

None of these make you a bad person. They're survival lessons your mind learned. But some of them are keeping you on the wrong side of money.

Scarcity vs. ownership thinking

Scarcity saysOwnership says
"I can't afford it.""How can I afford it?"
"Investing is for rich people.""I'll start with what I have."
"I'll deal with it later.""I'll deal with it now, a little at a time."
"How much is the monthly payment?""What's the total cost, and what does it produce?"
"Money comes and goes.""Money comes, and I tell it where to go."

The three stages: awareness, decision, control

Awareness is seeing clearly: your beliefs, your numbers, your habits. You started that in your reflections. Decision is choosing a different direction on purpose. Control is building systems so the right choice happens automatically, even on the days you don't feel motivated.

Your Right Side of Money control system

  1. Know your numbers. Your net worth, your monthly income, your monthly spending, and your total debt. Review them once a month.
  2. Pay yourself first. On payday, before any bill, move a set amount to savings and investing automatically.
  3. Protect what you're building. An emergency fund, the right insurance for your family, and a will or beneficiary designations so what you build goes where you intend.
  4. Eliminate expensive debt. Use the plan from Day 2 until high-interest debt is gone.
  5. Own something. Start with one ownership step and add to it every month.
  6. Grow your income. A raise, a certification, a side business. More income plus the same habits equals faster progress.
  7. Keep learning. Read one money book a quarter. Surround yourself with people who talk about building, not just buying.
"You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems."James Clear, Atomic Habits

Right Side move

Write down the money belief you heard most growing up. Under it, write whether it's helping you or hurting you today. If it's hurting you, write a new belief to replace it and read it every morning for 30 days. Then set up one automatic transfer, however small, to put that new belief into action.

Lesson 5 of 6

Why This Leads Directly to Cryptocurrency

Everything you've learned in three days, from fiat money to bailouts to inflation, sets the stage for the biggest experiment in money in our lifetime. Here's how it all connects.

January 3, 2009

Months after Lehman Brothers collapsed and governments rushed to bail out banks, a new kind of money went live. Its creator, known only by the name Satoshi Nakamoto, embedded a newspaper headline into the very first block of data:

"The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."

It was a timestamp. Many people also read it as a message about why Bitcoin was created.

Connecting the three days

Remember the pattern from the Money Timeline: money starts out scarce, paper promises replace it, the issuers create more promises than they can back, a crisis hits, and people go looking for something new to trust. Cryptocurrency is the latest step in that pattern. Here's how it responds to each problem you've studied:

What you learnedWhat Bitcoin was designed to do
Fiat money has no hard limit on supply (Day 1)Bitcoin has a fixed maximum of 21 million coins, written into its code.
Central banks and governments control the money (Day 1)No central bank or company controls it. A worldwide network of computers maintains it.
Banks create money through lending and profit from the spread (Day 2)You can hold and send it directly, without a bank in the middle.
Inflation and QE quietly dilute savers (Day 2)New coins are released on a fixed schedule that gets cut in half roughly every four years, called the "halving."
Bailouts rescue big institutions first (Day 3)No one can create more to rescue anyone.

The key concepts in plain English

Blockchain
A public ledger shared across thousands of computers. Every transaction is recorded and very hard to change after the fact.
Wallet and keys
Your crypto is controlled by a private key, like a master password. Whoever holds the key controls the coins.
Self-custody
Holding your own keys instead of leaving coins on an exchange. More control, more responsibility.
Stablecoins
Digital tokens designed to stay equal to a dollar, backed by reserves. In 2025, Congress passed the GENIUS Act to set federal rules for them.
CBDC
A central bank digital currency: a digital dollar issued by a central bank. The opposite of Bitcoin's design, and heavily debated.

From fringe to mainstream

In January 2024, U.S. regulators approved spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which let people own Bitcoin through a regular brokerage account. Major financial firms that once dismissed crypto now offer it to their clients. Whatever you think of it, it's no longer a fringe idea.

Now the other side: the real risks

Understanding comes before investing. Cryptocurrency carries serious risks, and the Right Side of Money approach is to look at them head-on.

  • Extreme volatility. Bitcoin fell roughly 75% from its late-2021 high to its late-2022 low. Many smaller coins went to zero.
  • Scams and fraud. Fake coins, "guaranteed return" schemes, romance scams, and impersonators have cost people billions. Anyone promising guaranteed crypto returns is lying.
  • Platform failures. In November 2022, the exchange FTX collapsed and customers' funds were frozen. If you don't hold your keys, you're trusting a company.
  • Lost keys. Lose your private key, and your coins are gone forever. There's no customer service line.
  • Taxes. The IRS treats crypto as property. Selling, trading, or spending it can trigger a taxable gain. Keep records.
  • No cash flow. Critics point out that Bitcoin doesn't produce earnings like a business or rent like property. Its price depends entirely on what others will pay.

Right Side of Money crypto rules

  1. Build your foundation first: emergency fund, high-interest debt gone, retirement investing started.
  2. Only invest money you can afford to lose completely.
  3. Keep it a small part of your total investments.
  4. Learn how it works before you buy. If you can't explain it, don't own it.
  5. Never share your private keys or seed phrase with anyone, ever.
  6. Ignore hype, influencers, and anything that promises guaranteed returns.

Why does this program end here? Because cryptocurrency forces the questions you've been learning to ask: What is money? Who controls it? What gives it value? Who benefits first? Whether crypto becomes the future of money or a chapter in its history, you now have the framework to judge it for yourself instead of relying on hype or fear.

Right Side move

Before you ever buy any cryptocurrency, write one paragraph answering: What problem does this solve? Who controls it? What's the maximum supply? What would make it go to zero? If you can't answer all four, you're not investing. You're gambling.

Day 3 · Right Side of Money Takeaway

You can't control the system. You can control your position in it.

Today you learned how people come out of every crisis stronger. Here's what you should carry for the rest of your life.

Workers earn money. Owners build wealth. The system rewards ownership, so become an owner.

What you now know

  1. Every crisis follows a pattern: easy money, leverage, euphoria, a trigger, panic, rescue, recovery. Low debt, cash reserves, and patience turn crises into opportunities.
  2. The income tax and the Fed were both born in 1913, and the tax code is written to reward investors, business owners, and property owners.
  3. Ownership is the dividing line of wealth. Assets produce income or grow. Liabilities cost you. Start owning, even small, and automate it.
  4. Financial literacy wasn't taught to most of us, but it's spreading, and it works. You're now the one who can teach it to your family.
  5. Mindset drives behavior. Recognize your money scripts, choose new beliefs, and build systems that act on them automatically.
  6. Cryptocurrency is a response to the problems of fiat money. It offers fixed supply and independence from banks, and it carries serious risks. Understand before you invest.
