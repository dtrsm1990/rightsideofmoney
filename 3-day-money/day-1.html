Lesson 1 of 6

The Origin of Money

Money wasn't invented by a bank or a government. People created it to solve a problem, and that problem still explains how money works today.

Picture a small village thousands of years ago. You raise goats. Your neighbor grows wheat. Another neighbor makes pottery. You want some wheat, but the wheat farmer doesn't need a goat right now. He needs a pot. The potter doesn't want your goat either. Everybody has something of value, but nobody can get what they need.

Economists call this the double coincidence of wants. For a straight trade to work, each person has to want exactly what the other person has, at the same time, in the right amount. That almost never happens. And how do you split a goat to buy a small bag of wheat?

The first solutions were memory and credit

For a long time, textbooks taught that people bartered first and invented money later. Many anthropologists now believe the real story is a little different. In small communities, people mostly kept track of who owed what. You give me wheat today, I owe you a favor or a goat later. Money started as a way of keeping score.

In ancient Mesopotamia, around 3000 BCE, temple and palace officials recorded debts on clay tablets. Debts were measured in barley or in silver weighed by a unit called the shekel. Around 1754 BCE, the Code of Hammurabi, one of the oldest written legal codes, included rules about loans and interest. So debt and interest are older than coins. Keep that in mind. It comes back on Day 2.

Commodity money: things with their own value

As trade grew between strangers who didn't trust each other, people started using items everyone wanted. This is called commodity money. It has value on its own, even if it isn't used as money.

  • Grain and cattle in farming societies. The word "pecuniary," meaning related to money, comes from the Latin pecus, meaning cattle.
  • Salt, which preserved food and was valuable across the ancient world.
  • Cowrie shells, used as money in parts of Asia and Africa for centuries.
  • Silver and gold, which became the favorites almost everywhere.

Why gold and silver won

Over time, societies discovered that the best money has certain qualities. Gold and silver scored high on nearly all of them.

Durable
It doesn't rot, rust, or die like grain or cattle.
Portable
A lot of value fits in a small space.
Divisible
You can break it into smaller units to make change.
Uniform
One ounce is as good as any other ounce.
Scarce
Nobody can create more of it overnight.
Accepted
People trust that others will take it.

Coins, paper, and receipts

Around 600 BCE, the kingdom of Lydia, in what's now Turkey, stamped some of the first known coins. A stamp from the king meant nobody had to weigh the metal each time. That was a big idea: trust in the issuer replaced checking the metal.

China went further. By the early 1000s, the Song dynasty was issuing official paper money. In Europe, by the 1600s, merchants stored gold with goldsmiths and received paper receipts. People started trading the receipts instead of the gold. Goldsmiths noticed that most people never came back for their gold at the same time, so they began lending out more receipts than they had gold behind them. That's the seed of modern banking, and you'll see where it leads on Day 2.

The three jobs money does

Whatever form it takes, money does three jobs. If something does all three well, it works as money.

Medium of exchange
You can trade it for goods and services. It solves the double coincidence of wants.
Unit of account
It's a common measuring stick. Everything gets priced in the same unit, so you can compare.
Store of value
It holds its buying power over time, so you can save today and spend later.

Watch that third job closely. Modern money is very good at the first two jobs. How well it does the third job is one of the biggest questions in this whole program.

Right Side move

Next time you pay for something, pause for one second and ask: "What am I really trading for this?" The answer is your time. If you earn $25 an hour, a $100 purchase costs you four hours of your life. Money is stored time. Spend it like it matters.

Lesson 2 of 6

What Gives Money Value

A $100 bill costs less than 20 cents to print. So why will a stranger hand you $100 worth of groceries for it?

Money has gone through three basic stages. Understanding them explains almost everything about the dollar in your pocket.

TypeWhat it isWhere the value comes from
Commodity moneyThe money is the valuable thing itself: gold coins, silver, salt.The material. Melt the coin down and you still have gold.
Representative moneyPaper that can be exchanged for a set amount of a commodity, like a gold certificate.The promise that you can redeem it for gold or silver.
Fiat moneyMoney with no backing by a physical commodity. Every major currency today is fiat, including the U.S. dollar.Trust, law, and the strength of the economy behind it.

The word fiat is Latin for "let it be done." In other words, it's money because the government says so. That sounds shaky at first. But fiat money has real support behind it. It rests on four pillars.

Pillar 1: Trust

You accept dollars because you're confident the next person will accept them from you. Your landlord takes them because the grocery store takes them. The grocery store takes them because its suppliers take them. The whole system runs on a shared belief that tomorrow, dollars will still work. When that trust breaks, money can collapse fast.

Pillar 2: Law

Look at any U.S. bill. It says: "This note is legal tender for all debts, public and private." Legal tender means it's legally valid for paying debts. Even more important, the government requires you to pay your taxes in dollars. Every working person and business in the country needs dollars to settle with the IRS. That creates constant demand for the currency.

Pillar 3: Productivity

A dollar is really a claim on the goods, services, and labor of the economy that issues it. The U.S. dollar is backed by the output of hundreds of millions of workers, millions of businesses, and a large, stable economy. When you hold dollars, you hold a ticket to trade for what all those people produce.

Pillar 4: Controlled supply

Fiat money only holds value if it stays relatively scarce. Since there's no gold limiting how much can be created, the people in charge have to show restraint. When they don't, you get inflation. When they really don't, you get hyperinflation.

When the pillars crack

Germany, 1923. After World War I, the German government printed money to cover its obligations. Prices doubled every few days. People were paid twice a day so they could spend their wages before they lost value. Life savings were wiped out.

Zimbabwe, 2008. The government printed money to cover spending while the economy was shrinking. Inflation reached levels so extreme that the country issued a 100 trillion dollar note. It couldn't buy a week of groceries. In 2009, Zimbabwe stopped using its own currency.

In both cases, paper was still paper. What disappeared was trust, productivity, and restraint.

So is the dollar "fake"?

No. It's better to say the dollar is a system built on agreement. That system has worked remarkably well for decades, and the U.S. dollar is the world's main reserve currency. But agreements can be managed well or poorly. The value of every dollar you earn and save depends on decisions made by people you've probably never met. That's why you need to understand how those decisions get made.

Fiat money
Currency that has value because of government decree and public trust, not because it can be exchanged for a commodity.
Legal tender
Money that the law recognizes as valid for paying debts.
Hyperinflation
Extremely fast, out-of-control price increases, often above 50% per month.

Right Side move

Say this out loud once: "The dollar is only as strong as the trust and discipline behind it." Then ask yourself how much of your wealth is sitting only in dollars. You don't have to change anything today. Just notice. By Day 3, you'll know what to do with that answer.

Lesson 3 of 6

The Gold Standard

For most of American history, the dollar was tied to gold. Here's how that link worked, why it was broken, and what it means for you today.

Under a gold standard, a country promises that its currency can be exchanged for a fixed amount of gold. If the government says one ounce of gold equals $20.67, then anyone holding $20.67 could, in theory, trade it for an ounce. The government can only issue as much currency as its gold reserves can support.

How the U.S. got on gold

The Coinage Act of 1792 created the U.S. dollar and defined it in both gold and silver. Over the 1800s, the country leaned more and more on gold. The Gold Standard Act of 1900 made it official: the dollar was defined in gold, at $20.67 per ounce.

The strengths

  • Discipline. Governments couldn't create money out of nothing. They needed gold.
  • Long-term price stability. Over long stretches, prices stayed relatively steady.
  • Trust. People could see and hold what backed their money.

The weaknesses

  • Rigid supply. The money supply grew only when new gold was mined, not when the economy needed it.
  • Painful busts. In a crisis, the government couldn't easily add money to calm things down. Bank panics and deflation were common.
  • Gold runs. If people feared the dollar, they rushed to swap it for gold, which could drain reserves.

1933: Americans turn in their gold

During the Great Depression, banks were failing and people were hoarding gold. In April 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 6102. It required Americans to turn in most gold coins, gold bullion, and gold certificates to the Federal Reserve at $20.67 an ounce. There were limited exceptions for things like jewelry and rare coins. Breaking the order could bring a fine of up to $10,000 or up to ten years in prison.

Then, in January 1934, the Gold Reserve Act changed the official price of gold to $35 an ounce. Think about what that meant. People who turned in gold received $20.67 an ounce. A few months later, the government valued that same ounce at $35. The dollar had lost about 41% of its value measured in gold, and ordinary citizens took the loss.

1944: The dollar becomes the world's anchor

Near the end of World War II, 44 nations met at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. They built a new global system. Other currencies were pegged to the U.S. dollar, and the dollar was pegged to gold at $35 an ounce. Foreign central banks, but not ordinary Americans, could exchange their dollars for U.S. gold. The dollar became the world's reserve currency.

1971: The gold window closes

By the late 1960s, the U.S. was spending heavily on the Vietnam War and on programs at home. It had issued far more dollars than it had gold to back them. Foreign governments noticed and started asking for gold. U.S. gold reserves were shrinking.

On Sunday, August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon went on television and announced that the U.S. would suspend the dollar's convertibility into gold. This became known as the "Nixon Shock." It was described as temporary. It was never reversed. By 1973, major currencies were floating, meaning their values were set by markets instead of gold.

From that moment on, the U.S. dollar has been pure fiat money. Nothing physical limits how many dollars can be created. In 1974, Americans were allowed to own gold again, but as an investment, not as money.

$20.67Official price of one ounce of gold in 1933
$35Official price after the Gold Reserve Act of 1934
1971The year the last link between the dollar and gold was cut

Why this matters to you

Before 1971, there was a hard limit on how much money could be created. After 1971, the limit is a decision made by people. That's not automatically good or bad. But it means the value of your savings now depends on how well those decisions are made. The rest of this program shows you how they're made and how to protect yourself.

Right Side move

Ask the oldest person in your family what things cost when they were young: a gallon of gas, a loaf of bread, a movie ticket. Then compare it to today. You'll be looking at more than 50 years of fiat money in one conversation, and you'll never think about "saving cash" the same way again.

Lesson 4 of 6

The Creation of the Federal Reserve

It's one of the most powerful institutions in the world, and most people can't explain what it does. By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to.

October 1907

A failed attempt to corner the copper market sets off a chain reaction in New York. Depositors panic and line up to pull their money out of the Knickerbocker Trust Company, one of the city's largest trust companies. It fails. Panic spreads. Stock prices crash. Banks across the country refuse to pay out cash.

There's no central bank to step in. So the country turns to one man: banker J.P. Morgan. He gathers the leading bankers in his library and pressures them to pool money to stop the collapse. It works, barely. But the lesson is clear: the most powerful economy in the world is depending on one private banker in a crisis.

The road to 1913

After the Panic of 1907, Congress set up the National Monetary Commission to study banking systems around the world. In November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich and a small group of powerful bankers met in secret at the Jekyll Island Club, off the coast of Georgia, to draft a plan for an American central bank. They traveled under first names only to avoid attention.

Their plan was debated and reshaped for three years. On December 23, 1913, President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act. The Federal Reserve System, often just called "the Fed," was born.

How the Fed is built

Board of Governors
Seven members in Washington, D.C., appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms.
12 regional Reserve Banks
Located in cities like New York, Chicago, Atlanta, and San Francisco. They serve banks in their region.
FOMC
The Federal Open Market Committee. It sets interest rate policy, which affects every loan in the country.

Here's a detail most people never learn. The regional Reserve Banks are technically owned by the commercial banks in their districts, which are required to buy stock in them. But that stock doesn't work like normal stock. It pays a limited dividend and doesn't give control. After expenses, the Fed sends most of its profits to the U.S. Treasury. So the Fed is best described as an independent part of the government with a private-style structure. That unusual setup is still debated today.

What the Fed actually does

  1. Sets monetary policy. It raises or lowers a key short-term interest rate called the federal funds rate. That ripples out to credit cards, car loans, mortgages, and savings accounts.
  2. Acts as lender of last resort. In a panic, it can lend to banks so a run doesn't take down the whole system. This was the exact job nobody could do in 1907.
  3. Supervises banks. It helps oversee the safety of the banking system.
  4. Runs the payment plumbing. It helps move trillions of dollars between banks.

One common myth: the Fed doesn't physically print dollar bills. The Bureau of Engraving and Printing, part of the Treasury, prints them. The Fed orders and distributes them. But as you'll see tomorrow, most new money isn't printed at all.

The dual mandate

In 1977, Congress gave the Fed its main goals. The law lists maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. The first two are known as the dual mandate:

  • Maximum employment: as many people working as the economy can support.
  • Stable prices: low, steady inflation. Since 2012, the Fed has officially aimed for 2% inflation per year.

Those two goals often pull against each other. Cutting interest rates can boost jobs but risk higher inflation. Raising rates can cool inflation but can cost jobs. Every time you hear "the Fed raised rates" or "the Fed cut rates," they're trying to balance these two goals.

Did it work?

The Fed was created to prevent panics. Yet less than 20 years later, the Great Depression hit and thousands of banks failed. Many economists, including Nobel Prize winner Milton Friedman, argued the Fed made the Depression worse by letting the money supply shrink. The Fed has learned a lot since then. But it's run by people, and people make mistakes. That's why you should understand it instead of blindly trusting it.

Right Side move

The Fed's rate-setting committee meets eight times a year. Look up the next meeting date and put it on your calendar. When the decision comes out, check your credit card rate and your savings rate over the following weeks. You'll start seeing the Fed's decisions show up in your own accounts.

Lesson 5 of 6

Money Supply

How much money exists? It depends on what you count. And when that number jumps, your life gets more expensive.

The money supply is the total amount of money available in an economy. Economists measure it in layers, from the most spendable money to money that takes a step or two to reach. These layers are called monetary aggregates.

MeasureWhat it includesThink of it as
Monetary basePhysical currency in circulation plus the reserves banks keep at the Fed.The raw material the Fed controls directly.
M1Cash in people's hands, checking accounts, and other deposits you can spend or move right away. Since 2020, regular savings accounts count here too.Money ready to spend today.
M2Everything in M1, plus small time deposits like CDs under $100,000 and retail money market funds.Spendable money plus "near money."

A change most textbooks miss

Older textbooks say M2 is where savings accounts show up. In May 2020, the Federal Reserve changed its definitions and moved regular savings accounts into M1, because people can now move that money almost instantly. CDs and money market funds are still counted only in M2.

2020 to 2022: the money supply surge

When the pandemic hit, the government and the Fed moved fast. There were stimulus checks, expanded unemployment benefits, business loans, and massive Fed support for markets. In March 2020, the Fed also cut bank reserve requirements to zero.

26.9%M2 growth in the year ending February 2021, the fastest in modern records
About 40%How much M2 grew from early 2020 to its 2022 peak
9.1%Inflation in June 2022, the highest in about 40 years

Look at the order of events: first, a flood of new money. Then, about a year later, prices started climbing fast. That's not a coincidence. When the amount of money grows much faster than the amount of goods and services, each dollar ends up chasing fewer things. Sellers raise prices.

Economists still debate how much of the 2021 to 2022 inflation came from the money supply and how much came from supply chain problems, energy prices, and shifts in spending. Most agree the money surge played a real role.

Velocity: how fast money moves

There's one more piece. Velocity is how often each dollar changes hands in a year. If people are scared and hold onto cash, velocity drops and new money doesn't push prices up much right away. When people start spending again, velocity picks up and that stored-up money hits the economy. That helps explain the delay between the 2020 money surge and the 2022 price spike.

Money supply
The total amount of money in an economy at a given time.
Liquidity
How quickly something can be turned into spendable cash without losing value.
Velocity of money
How many times the average dollar is spent in a year.

Right Side move

Sort your own money the way the Fed does. Write down how much you have in cash and checking (money ready now) and how much is in CDs, money market funds, or other near money. Then ask: is my ready money earning anything at all? Most checking accounts pay almost nothing. That's the first leak we'll fix.

The pattern

Step back and look at the whole thing. You'll see one pattern repeat again and again:

  1. Money starts out tied to something scarce.
  2. Paper promises replace the scarce thing because they're more convenient.
  3. The people issuing the promises create more promises than they can back.
  4. A crisis hits, and the rules get rewritten, usually in favor of the issuer.
  5. People go looking for something new they can trust.

That pattern isn't history. It's still happening. And it's the reason Day 3 ends where it does.

Day 1 · Right Side of Money Takeaway

Money is an agreement. Learn the rules of the agreement.

Today you traced money from clay tablets to digital dollars. Here's what you should carry with you.

The dollar isn't backed by gold. It's backed by trust, law, and the work of millions of people, and managed by people you'll never meet.

What you now know

  1. Money was created to solve a problem: trading between people who don't have exactly what the other wants. Its three jobs are medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value.
  2. Today's dollar is fiat money. Its value rests on trust, legal tender laws, the productivity of the economy, and discipline in how much gets created.
  3. The link to gold was cut in steps: 1933 for citizens, 1971 for everyone else. Since then, there's no hard limit on how many dollars can exist.
  4. The Federal Reserve was created in 1913 after the Panic of 1907. Its dual mandate is maximum employment and stable prices.
  5. The money supply is measured in layers. When it grows much faster than the economy, prices tend to follow.

The Right Side of Money difference

People on the wrong side of money only ask, "How much do I make?" People on the right side also ask, "What is my money, who controls it, and what happens to it while I'm holding it?" You started asking those questions today. That's the shift.
