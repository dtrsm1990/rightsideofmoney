Day 1 | Lesson 1

Money, Trust, and Why Crypto Exists

Before you buy a single coin, understand the problem it was built to solve.

Every form of money runs on one thing: trust. A dollar bill is paper. It's worth something because we trust the government that issues it, the banks that hold it, and each other to accept it. For most of history, someone in the middle kept the record of who owns what: a bank, a government, a payment company.

Cryptocurrency asks a radical question: what if nobody had to be in the middle? What if a network of computers, following the same rules, could keep the record of who owns what, and nobody could change it on their own?

How We Got Here

DateWhat HappenedWhy It Matters
2008The global financial crisis. Banks fail, governments bail them out, and trust in the system takes a hit.Bitcoin was born in that moment of distrust.
October 31, 2008Someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto publishes a nine-page paper describing "a peer-to-peer electronic cash system."Nobody has ever confirmed who Satoshi is.
January 3, 2009The first block of the Bitcoin network is created. Embedded in it is a newspaper headline about a second bank bailout.The message was intentional: this was money outside the banking system.
May 22, 2010A programmer pays 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas, one of the first real-world purchases.At bitcoin's 2025 peak, those coins were worth over a billion dollars.
2015Ethereum launches, adding programmable contracts to the blockchain idea.Crypto becomes a platform, not just a currency.
2024 to 2025U.S. regulators approve spot bitcoin and ether funds (ETFs), and Congress passes the first federal stablecoin law.Crypto moves into retirement accounts and the mainstream financial system.

What Crypto Promises

  • No middleman required. You can send value to anyone, anywhere, without a bank's permission.
  • A fixed or predictable supply. Bitcoin's supply is capped at 21 million coins. No one can print more.
  • Ownership you control. If you hold your own keys, nobody can freeze your account.
  • A public record. Every transaction is recorded on a ledger anyone can inspect.

What Those Promises Cost

Every one of those strengths has a flip side, and this program will never show you one without the other:

The PromiseThe Flip Side
No middlemanNo one to call when something goes wrong. No fraud department. No chargebacks.
You control your moneyYou alone are responsible for protecting it. Lose your keys and your money is gone for good.
Transactions are finalSend to the wrong address or to a scammer and there's no undo button.
Outside the traditional systemNo FDIC insurance. No SIPC protection. Fewer of the safeguards you're used to.

The Core Truth

Crypto is a new kind of financial technology, not a get-rich-quick button. Some of it may change how money works. A lot of it is speculation, and some of it is fraud. Your job over these five days is to learn to tell the difference, and to protect yourself while you do.

Real-Life Case Study: Two Brothers, Same Headline

In early 2021, Jerome and his brother Malik both saw headlines about bitcoin passing $50,000. Malik bought $4,000 worth that week on a tip from a coworker, without knowing what he owned or why. Jerome spent a month learning first, then started with $50 a month.

When prices fell more than 70% in 2022, Malik panicked and sold for about $1,100. Jerome kept his small, steady plan running because he'd expected volatility from the start. Same asset. Same years. The difference wasn't the coin. It was the understanding.

Think About This Before Moving On

Why are you interested in crypto right now? Is it the technology, the headlines, a friend's success, or fear of missing out? Be honest. You'll write your answer in today's Action Exercise, and it matters more than you think.

How a Blockchain Actually Works

You don't need to be a programmer. You do need to understand what you're trusting.

A blockchain is a shared record book. Instead of one bank keeping the ledger, thousands of computers around the world each keep a copy. When someone sends crypto, the transaction is broadcast to the network, checked, and added to everyone's copy at the same time.

The Five Building Blocks

  1. Transactions. "Send 0.01 bitcoin from this address to that address." Each one is signed with the sender's private key, proving it's really them.
  2. Blocks. Transactions are grouped into blocks. On Bitcoin, a new block is added about every 10 minutes.
  3. Hashes. Each block gets a digital fingerprint called a hash. Change one character in the block and the fingerprint changes completely.
  4. The chain. Each block includes the fingerprint of the block before it. Tampering with an old block would break every block after it, and the network would reject it.
  5. Consensus. The network agrees on which blocks are valid using a set of rules, so no single person decides.

Try changing $50 to $500. The fingerprint changes completely. That's why records on a blockchain are so hard to fake: any change shows up instantly.

Who Keeps the Network Honest?

Proof of Work (Bitcoin)Proof of Stake (Ethereum and many others)
How blocks are added"Miners" use powerful computers to solve a math puzzle. The winner adds the block and earns new coins plus fees."Validators" lock up, or stake, their own coins as collateral. They're chosen to add blocks and earn rewards.
What stops cheatingCheating would require more computing power than the rest of the network combined.Validators who cheat can lose part of their staked coins.
Energy useVery high. Bitcoin mining uses as much electricity as some countries.Low. Ethereum cut its energy use by roughly 99.9% when it switched in September 2022.

Confirmations and Finality

When you send crypto, it isn't "done" the instant you tap send. It's done when it's included in a block, and safer with each block added after it. Exchanges often wait for several confirmations before crediting a deposit. Once confirmed, a transaction can't be reversed by anyone. Not you, not the exchange, not the police.

Public Keys, Private Keys, and Addresses

  • Your address is like an account number. You can share it so people can send you crypto.
  • Your private key is the master password that proves you own what's at that address. Whoever has it controls the money.
  • Your recovery phrase (usually 12 or 24 words) can rebuild your private keys. It's the most valuable thing you'll ever write down.

Right Side of Money Principle

On a blockchain, whoever holds the keys owns the money. That single sentence explains most crypto thefts, most lost fortunes, and most of what you'll learn on Day 3.

Bitcoin, Ethereum, Stablecoins, and Everything Else

"Crypto" isn't one thing. It's thousands of very different things wearing the same name.

When someone says they "invested in crypto," that could mean they bought bitcoin, or it could mean they bought a cartoon-dog token created last Tuesday. Those aren't the same risk. Not even close.

Bitcoin: Digital Scarcity

  • Purpose: A digital form of money and store of value that no government or company controls.
  • Supply: Capped at 21 million coins. About 95% have already been created.
  • The halving: About every four years, the reward miners earn for new blocks is cut in half. The most recent halving was in April 2024, and the next is expected around 2028.
  • Why people own it: Many treat it as "digital gold": scarce, portable, and hard to seize.

Ethereum: A Programmable Platform

  • Purpose: A global computer that runs "smart contracts," programs that execute automatically when conditions are met.
  • Ether (ETH) is the coin used to pay for activity on the network. Those payments are called gas fees.
  • What's built on it: Stablecoins, decentralized finance (DeFi) apps, digital collectibles (NFTs), and thousands of other tokens.

Stablecoins: Crypto Pegged to a Dollar

Stablecoins are tokens designed to stay worth $1. They're used to move dollars on blockchains quickly. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, created the first federal rules for U.S. payment stablecoins. Once its rules take effect, by January 2027 at the latest, issuers must hold at least $1 of safe reserves, like cash and Treasury bills, for every $1 issued, and can't pay interest to holders.

Two warnings. First, stablecoins have no FDIC insurance; the law says so directly. Second, not every coin that calls itself "stable" is. In May 2022, TerraUSD, an "algorithmic" stablecoin backed mainly by its own sister token rather than by cash, collapsed to near zero, and tens of billions of dollars in value vanished in days.

Bitcoin alone has often been more than half the value of the entire crypto market. Meanwhile, millions of tokens have been created on launch websites, and the vast majority of them are now worthless or abandoned.

Real-Life Case Study: Danielle and the "Next Bitcoin"

Danielle saw a video promising a new token would be "the next bitcoin" and put $1,500 in. The price tripled in three days. On day four, the creators sold their huge share of the supply and the price fell 98% in an hour. That's called a rug pull. Her $1,500 became about $30.

When she looked back, every warning sign was there: anonymous creators, no real purpose, most of the supply held by a few wallets, and a countdown pushing people to "get in early."

Right Side of Money Principle

Before you ever say "I invest in crypto," be able to say exactly which crypto, what it does, and why it should exist in ten years. If you can't, you're not investing yet. You're guessing.

Day 1 | Lesson 4

What Gives Crypto Value, and What Doesn't

A stock is a piece of a business. A coin is something else. Know the difference.

When you own a share of stock, you own part of a company with sales, profits, and assets. You can estimate what it's worth from what it earns. Most crypto doesn't produce earnings. Bitcoin doesn't pay dividends. There's no company behind it with a profit-and-loss statement. So what makes it worth anything?

Where Crypto's Value Comes From

  • Scarcity: A limited, predictable supply that can't be inflated away.
  • Network effects: The more people use and accept it, the more useful it becomes, like a phone network.
  • Utility: Some networks are used for real activity: sending money, settling trades, running applications.
  • Security and track record: Bitcoin has run continuously since 2009 without its core ledger being successfully rewritten.
  • Belief and demand: In the end, the price is what the next buyer will pay. That's powerful when belief grows, and brutal when it fades.

The Number That Matters Isn't the Price

Beginners look at the price per coin. Professionals look at market capitalization, the total value of all the coins:

Market Cap = Price Per Coin x Number of Coins

This is why a "cheap" coin isn't a bargain. A token priced at $0.01 with 500 trillion coins in circulation is already "worth" $5 trillion. For that token to reach $1, it would need to be worth $500 trillion, several times more than the entire world economy produces in a year. It's not going to happen. The price of one coin tells you almost nothing. Supply tells you the rest.

Common BeliefThe Reality
"It's only a penny, so it can easily go to $1."Whether it can depends entirely on how many coins exist. Most "penny coins" have supplies in the billions or trillions.
"I can only afford a fraction of a bitcoin, so it's not for me."You can buy a fraction. $25 of bitcoin is fine. Owning a whole coin doesn't matter.
"It went up 1,000% last year, so it's a winner."Past gains say nothing about future value. Many coins that rose 1,000% later fell more than 95%.
"A celebrity is promoting it, so it must be legit."Celebrities have been sued and fined for promoting crypto they were paid to push.

Crypto can be any of the three. Which one you're doing depends on you, not the coin. By Day 5, you'll have written rules that keep you on the investing side of that table.

A Sober Benchmark

Even bitcoin, the largest and most established crypto asset, peaked near $126,000 in October 2025 and fell to around $59,000 by June 2026, a drop of more than 50% in eight months. Smaller coins usually fall much further in the same periods. Expect volatility. Plan for it. Day 2 shows you exactly how.

Right Side of Money Principle

Value you can't explain is value you can't hold. If the only reason you own something is that it's going up, you'll sell it the moment it goes down.
